# Reasonable Accommodations at Work: A Guide for Companies in Mexico

**Direct answer:** a reasonable accommodation at work is a necessary and appropriate modification that enables a person with a disability to apply for a job, perform their duties, receive training, participate, and advance on equal terms, without imposing a disproportionate or undue burden. To manage it well, a company needs an accessible channel, a conversation focused on barriers and job functions, individual assessment, documented decision-making, confidentiality, and follow-up.

No catalogue exists that allows every case to be answered with a simple "yes" or "no." The same solution may work for one person and be useless for another. That is why a serious process replaces guesswork with dialogue, testing, and evidence.

> A reasonable accommodation does not change the essential standard of the role. It removes a barrier that was interfering with the ability to demonstrate it.

## What Reasonable Accommodations Are

[Article 2 of the Convention on the Rights of Persons with Disabilities](https://www.un.org/development/desa/disabilities/convention-on-the-rights-of-persons-with-disabilities/article-2-definitions.html) defines reasonable accommodations as necessary and appropriate modifications and adjustments, required in a particular case, that do not impose a disproportionate or undue burden and that enable the exercise of rights on an equal basis. The Convention also includes the denial of reasonable accommodations within the definition of discrimination on the basis of disability.

[Article 27 on work and employment](https://www.un.org/development/desa/disabilities/convention-on-the-rights-of-persons-with-disabilities/article-27-work-and-employment.html) recognizes the right to work in an open, inclusive, and accessible labor market and work environment. Among the measures identified is ensuring reasonable accommodations in the workplace.

In Mexico, the [General Law for the Inclusion of Persons with Disabilities](https://www.diputados.gob.mx/LeyesBiblio/pdf/LGIPD.pdf) forms part of the national framework on rights, accessibility, work, and inclusion. This guide is educational and operational in nature; it does not replace a legal review of a specific case or of the obligations applicable to each organization.

## Reasonable Accommodation, Accessibility, and Universal Design: What Is the Difference?

The three concepts complement one another, but they address different problems:

- **Accessibility:** broadly removes barriers so that facilities, information, technology, services, and processes can be used.
- **Universal design:** seeks to ensure that an environment, product, or process works for the greatest number of people from the outset.
- **Reasonable accommodation:** responds to an individual need that persists in a specific situation.

A video-call system with subtitles available to all staff improves accessibility. Activating Mexican Sign Language interpretation for a specific training session may be an accommodation. Designing an onboarding process from the start with materials in multiple formats reduces the number of exceptions that will need to be improvised later.

The goal is not to replace general accessibility with a collection of patches. It is worth reviewing the entire environment first—including offices and commercial spaces using [universal design](https://inklusion-blog.vercel.app/blog/diseno-universal-oficinas-comercios-checklist-mexico/) criteria—and maintaining an individual process for what cannot be anticipated in advance.

## When an Accommodation May Be Needed

The need can arise before hiring and may change throughout the employment relationship. A procedure should cover, at a minimum:

- job postings and application forms;
- interviews, technical tests, and assessments;
- onboarding, induction, and training;
- tools, workspace, schedules, and day-to-day communication;
- performance reviews and promotion opportunities;
- travel, events, and team activities;
- changes in role, technology, or facilities;
- return to work or the onset of a disability during employment;
- emergency and safety protocols.

Waiting for the person to "push hard enough" is not a process. It also creates a bias: those with more confidence, information, or influence receive a response; those without end up adapting at the cost of their health, productivity, or continued employment.

## A Seven-Step Process for Managing a Request

### 1. Receive It Without Requiring Perfect Wording

A person should not have to memorize legal language to start the process. Phrases such as "I need another way to receive instructions," "the platform doesn't work with my screen reader," or "this schedule is creating a barrier related to my disability" should trigger a conversation.

Publish at least two accessible channels for requesting support—for example, a form and a direct contact—and indicate who is responsible, the response timeframe, and how the request will be handled confidentially.

### 2. Understand the Barrier and the Relevant Function

Separate three elements: what function must be performed, what barrier is present, and what outcome would enable participation. Avoid turning the conversation into an interrogation about the person's clinical history.

A useful question is: "What change or support would allow you to carry out this task?" Another is: "Is there more than one alternative we could try?" The goal is to understand functioning and context, not to collect diagnoses.

### 3. Explore Options Together with the Person

The person often knows solutions that already work for them, but they should not have to carry the entire design process alone. Human Resources, the direct manager, technology, facilities, safety, and specialists can all contribute alternatives. When an exact option is not feasible, analyze another that produces an equivalent outcome.

### 4. Evaluate Effectiveness, Safety, Timeline, and Resources

Compare options using consistent criteria: Does it remove the barrier? Does it preserve essential functions? Does it introduce risks? How long will it take? What maintenance does it require? What real resources does it demand? "We've never done it before" is not a criterion; it is merely a description of an ordinary Tuesday.

A disproportionate or undue burden should not be assumed on the basis of inconvenience, an isolated cost, or a department's preference. It requires analysis of the specific case and the organization's context. If there is a legal, privacy, or safety question, seek relevant advice before closing the request.

### 5. Decide and Document

Record the request, the options considered, the decision, those responsible, dates, and the review criterion. Communication must be clear and accessible. If an option is not approved, explain why and which alternatives will be evaluated; a one-line refusal is not a dialogue.

### 6. Implement Without Disclosing Unnecessary Information

Share with each area only what is necessary to carry out the accommodation. The team may need to know that a meeting will have subtitles or that an instruction will be provided in writing; they do not need to know a diagnosis. Define who purchases, configures, trains, and provides support.

### 7. Review Whether It Worked

Agreeing on a follow-up date prevents an accommodation left gathering dust in a box from being counted as a "resolved case." Ask whether the barrier decreased, whether the solution is being used, whether it maintains the person's autonomy, and whether the role or environment has changed. Adjusting an accommodation is not failure; it is management.

## Examples of Reasonable Accommodations at Work

The following examples open up possibilities; they do not assign an automatic solution based on a type of disability:

- **Communication:** written instructions, accessible documents, easy-read formats, subtitles, transcription, Mexican Sign Language interpretation, or additional time to process a response.
- **Technology:** screen reader, magnification, voice recognition, alternative keyboard, software compatibility, or correction of an inaccessible application.
- **Physical space:** workstation change, adjustable height, obstacle-free route, signage, lighting control, or a location near accessible facilities.
- **Organization:** flexible schedule, planned breaks, hybrid working when the function allows it, redistribution of peripheral tasks, or more predictable work sequences.
- **Recruitment and training:** interview in an accessible space, assessment in a compatible format, justified additional time, materials provided in advance, or an alternative way to demonstrate the same competency.
- **Supervision:** explicit priorities, structured feedback, advance agenda, noise reduction in meetings, or an agreed channel for clarifying instructions.
- **Emergencies:** visual or vibrating alerts, defined routes and supports, accessible drills, and a plan that does not remove the person's autonomy.

A simple solution can have a significant impact. It can also fail if applied without asking. Moving someone to a "more comfortable" area, for example, could isolate them from the team or worsen their evacuation route. Conversation first; furniture second.

## Common Mistakes That Turn the Process Into a Barrier

- **Requesting a diagnosis before listening to the need.** Sensitive information is collected before the barrier has even been defined.
- **Using a closed list.** If the solution does not appear on the menu, it seems impossible even when it is straightforward.
- **Making the request public.** The person ends up explaining their situation to every manager, vendor, and colleague.
- **Confusing equality with uniformity.** Treating everyone in exactly the same way can preserve a barrier that affects people differently.
- **Modifying essential functions without analyzing them.** Before deciding, distinguish the outcome the role requires from the historical way of producing it.
- **Approving without implementing.** An authorization without a purchase, configuration, date, or responsible party is just an optimistic email.
- **Not reviewing changes.** A new office, manager, or platform can render a previous accommodation useless.
- **Retaliating or penalizing performance evaluations.** Requesting support should not automatically reduce opportunities, visibility, or ratings.

## Who Does What Within the Company

- **Leadership:** approves policy, resources, responsibilities, and escalation criteria.
- **Human Resources:** receives or coordinates requests, protects information, documents, and follows up.
- **Managers:** clarify functions and context, implement agreements, and prevent retaliation.
- **Technology and facilities:** evaluate compatibility, safety, procurement, adaptation, and support.
- **Procurement:** incorporates accessibility requirements into vendor selection and avoids acquiring new barriers.
- **The person making the request:** explains the barrier, participates in exploring alternatives, and reports whether the solution works; they do not have to become an unpaid consultant for the entire organization.

It is advisable to designate a coordinating person. Without one, every request bounces between departments until the problem learns the org chart.

## What an Internal Policy Should Include

- purpose and scope covering both candidates and employees;
- clear definitions of disability, accessibility, and reasonable accommodation;
- accessible channels and formats for submitting a request;
- a primary responsible party and a backup;
- timeframes for acknowledgment, analysis, decision, and implementation;
- criteria for requesting only relevant information;
- confidentiality and restricted access to data;
- a process for comparing alternatives and evaluating undue burden;
- a mechanism for review, reconsideration, and reporting of retaliation;
- budget, procurement, support, and maintenance;
- periodic follow-up and process improvement.

The policy must be available in accessible formats and accompanied by case-based training. A PDF stored on an inaccessible intranet has a rather questionable sense of humor.

## A 30-Day Plan to Get Started

1. **Days 1–5:** designate a responsible person, review recent cases, and map how support is currently requested.
2. **Days 6–10:** define a short workflow, two accessible channels, timeframes, and confidentiality rules.
3. **Days 11–15:** review forms, the recruitment system, the intranet, and documentation. Use the [inclusive recruitment guide](https://inklusion-blog.vercel.app/blog/reclutamiento-incluyente-personas-discapacidad-mexico/) to cover the pre-hiring stage.
4. **Days 16–20:** train Human Resources and managers using simulated requests, including one option that is initially not feasible.
5. **Days 21–25:** create a fast-track route with technology, facilities, procurement, and safety.
6. **Days 26–30:** test the process with the participation of people with disabilities, correct friction points, and communicate where to request support.

In parallel, review whether internal digital tools meet basic [web accessibility](https://inklusion-blog.vercel.app/blog/accesibilidad-web-wcag-aa-mexico/) requirements. Not everything should be resolved as an individual exception.

## How to Measure Whether the Process Works

- time to first contact and time to decision;
- time between approval and implementation;
- percentage of requests with follow-up on the agreed date;
- percentage of accommodations that reduced the reported barrier;
- cases reopened due to implementation failures or environmental changes;
- satisfaction and perceived confidentiality among those who used the process;
- recurring barriers that should be addressed through general accessibility improvements;
- participation in training, promotion, and retention, analyzed with care and privacy.

Do not use a low number of requests as an automatic indicator of success. It may mean the environment is accessible; it may also mean that no one knows who to turn to or trusts the process enough to do so. Combine data with interviews, accessibility testing, and case review.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### What is a reasonable accommodation at work?

It is a necessary and appropriate modification or adjustment that enables a person with a disability to participate and exercise their rights on equal terms, provided it does not impose a disproportionate or undue burden. It is defined according to the barrier and the specific case.

### Who can request a reasonable accommodation?

A candidate or employee who encounters a barrier related to a disability can raise what change or support they need. The company must offer a clear, accessible, and confidential channel for receiving and analyzing the request.

### Does a reasonable accommodation always cost money?

No. Many accommodations involve changes to schedules, formats, communication, organization, or location. Others require technology, interpretation, or physical modifications. Cost must be analyzed alongside effectiveness, resources, alternatives, and real-world impact—not assumed.

### Can the company request medical information?

The conversation should focus on the barrier, the job functions, and the support needed. If additional information is required, only relevant information should be requested, it must be protected as confidential, and it must be handled in accordance with applicable regulations. For complex cases, it is advisable to obtain legal and specialist advice.

### Are accessibility and reasonable accommodation the same thing?

No. Accessibility and universal design seek to ensure that environments and processes work for many people from the outset. A reasonable accommodation addresses an individual need that persists in a specific case.

### How do you know whether an accommodation worked?

Agree on an observable outcome and a review date. The central question is whether the barrier decreased and whether the person can perform the relevant functions, participate, and advance on equal terms, without creating new risks.

## Conclusion

Managing reasonable accommodations is not about granting favors or improvising privileges. It is about distinguishing performance from barrier, having a conversation with the person, comparing options, and implementing a verifiable solution.

Start with something very concrete: publish a channel, designate a responsible person, and test the process with three realistic cases. Then use the requests as operational intelligence. If several people need the same adaptation, it may have ceased to be an exception and become an accessibility improvement that the entire company should adopt.
