# Accessible social media: a checklist for marketing teams

**Direct answer:** to create accessible social media, every post must communicate its information without relying solely on the image, audio, color, or a specific interface. The minimum workflow includes contextual alt text, reviewed captions, description of relevant visual elements, sufficient contrast, plain language, readable hashtags, and a review before publishing. For important content, also link to an accessible web page.

The most common mistake is treating accessibility as a field to fill in five seconds before publishing. By that point, the piece may already have tiny embedded text, a video whose information appears only on screen, and a carousel that functions as a document but without document structure.

> A campaign does not become accessible by writing "alt: image of people in an office." It becomes more accessible when someone can understand what that image communicates and why it is there.

## What does accessibility mean on social media?

It means reducing barriers so that people with different ways of seeing, hearing, processing, moving, or interacting can receive and understand the content. Not all barriers depend on the marketing team: LinkedIn, Instagram, Facebook, X, and other platforms control their own interfaces and change features frequently.

The organization does control important decisions: what information it places inside an image, whether it adds alt text, whether it reviews captions, how it uses color, how clear the message is, and whether an accessible alternative exists outside the social network.

The [Section508.gov guide on accessible social media](https://www.section508.gov/create/social-media/), updated in July 2026, recommends practices such as alt text, captions, audio description when visual information requires it, sufficient contrast, plain language, and capitalization of compound hashtags. Its legal obligations are directed at federal content in the United States; here we use its technical recommendations and do not claim that Section 508 applies generally to companies in Mexico.

## The accessible workflow starts before design

### 1. The brief defines what information is essential

Before choosing a format, write in one sentence what the audience must understand. If the answer is "the date, venue, and registration method," that information cannot live only inside a poster. Include it also as text in the post and on the landing page.

Ask whether the content needs to be an image. A quote, a job opening, or a short list may work better as native text. Design can accompany the message; it does not have to hold it hostage.

### 2. Design prepares the piece and its alternative

The deliverable should not be just a PNG or MP4 file. It should include proposed alt text, a transcript or caption file, carousel order, data descriptions, and the destination link. That way accessibility does not depend on the person publishing having to guess the content.

### 3. Community management publishes and verifies

After uploading the content, confirm that the alt text was saved, that captions appear and are synchronized, that the link works, and that the mobile view does not cut off information. A feature being available on the platform does not guarantee it was configured correctly.

## How do you write useful alt text?

Alt text communicates the purpose of the image in that context. The [W3C decision tree for images](https://www.w3.org/WAI/tutorials/images/decision-tree/) distinguishes between informative, functional, complex, redundant, and decorative images. That distinction avoids two extremes: omitting information or narrating every object without criteria.

### Describe meaning, not inventory

A photograph from an event may require: "Mariana López presents in front of a screen showing the quarterly results chart; three attendees follow the session with Mexican Sign Language interpretation." A name should only be included if it is verified and relevant. "Woman giving a talk" loses the context; describing every chair does too.

### Transcribe text that exists only in the image

If a card reads "Webinar, August 18, 10:00, free registration," that information must appear in the body of the post or in the alt text. For lengthy posters, the best approach is to move the information to an accessible page and use the post as an entry point.

### Explain charts and data

"Bar chart" does not communicate the finding. Summarize the trend and the important values: "Requests resolved on time rose from 62% in January to 84% in June." Publish a table or more complete description when precision is necessary.

### Do not repeat what is already next to the image

If the post explains all the information and the image is purely decorative, there is no need to duplicate the text. The options depend on each platform; when it allows marking content as decorative or leaving an empty alternative, use that judgment. If that option does not exist, a brief description can avoid noise.

## What does an accessible video need?

### Reviewed captions

W3C defines captions as a synchronized text version of speech and of the sound information necessary to understand the content. Its guide warns that [automatically generated captions are not sufficient without confirming their accuracy](https://www.w3.org/WAI/media/av/captions/). A single omitted "not" can reverse the message.

Review proper names, technical terms, punctuation, speaker turns, relevant sounds, and synchronization. Open captions—embedded in the video—are visible to everyone; closed captions can be turned on or off. When the platform allows it, offering closed captions gives the user control.

### Description of visual information

If the audio says "these are the results" while the screen shows a chart without explaining it, a person who cannot see the image loses the data. Integrate the description into the script: "Sales increased 12% and returns fell 4%." When that does not fit naturally, prepare a version with audio description or a descriptive transcript.

### Transcript and accessible link

A transcript helps those who prefer to read, are looking for a specific piece of information, or have a limited connection. For webinars, job openings, instructions, or announcements, host the full content on an accessible page and link to it from the post.

## Contrast, color, and text inside images

Section508.gov uses as a minimum reference a contrast ratio of 4.5:1 for normal text and 3:1 for large text, excluding certain logos or brand names. These values align with well-known WCAG criteria for text contrast, but a good piece also needs adequate size, spacing, and a noise-free background.

- Do not use color as the only indicator. Add text, labeled icons, or patterns.
- Avoid paragraphs inside an image. On mobile they end up as microscopic text.
- Maintain safe margins so the platform's interface does not cover captions or calls to action.
- Test the piece at actual phone size, not only zoomed in on the design screen.
- In carousels, number the slides and maintain a comprehensible sequence.

If a chart requires zooming in, verbal explanation, and goodwill to be understood, it probably needs another version.

## How do you write more accessible social text?

### Use plain language

Place the main idea at the beginning, develop one action per paragraph, and explain little-known acronyms. Plain language does not mean oversimplifying; it means avoiding making the audience do extra work to reconstruct the intent.

### Make links understandable

"Register for the accessibility webinar" is more informative than a line of "click here." On platforms that do not allow customizing link text, explain beforehand what the person will find and use a clean URL when possible.

### Capitalize compound hashtags

Write **#AccessibleSocialMedia** instead of **#accessiblesocialmedia**. An initial capital letter on each word makes visual separation and pronunciation easier. Limit the quantity; a wall of hashtags does not improve the message either.

### Use emojis in moderation

Screen readers announce emojis by their name. A long row can interrupt reading. Place them at natural pauses and avoid using them as repeated bullet points or as substitutes for essential words.

## Decisions by format

### Post with a single image

Include the essential information in the text, write a contextual alternative, check contrast, and confirm that the platform saved the description.

### Carousel or document

Publish a text summary, order and number the slides, avoid excessive text, and offer a link to an accessible HTML page. For documents, also consult the [accessible PDF checklist](https://inklusion-blog.vercel.app/blog/como-crear-pdf-accesible-checklist-empresas/); a downloadable carousel does not become accessible simply by being called a "document."

### Short video

Add reviewed captions, avoid fleeting text, verbalize visual data, and do not cover captions with controls. Check the result without sound and then with audio only.

### Event or job opening

Do not leave the date, location, requirements, or how to request accommodations only in the artwork. Lead to an accessible page. For job openings, the [inclusive recruitment guide](https://inklusion-blog.vercel.app/blog/reclutamiento-incluyente-personas-discapacidad-mexico/) helps review the entire process, not just the post.

## 12-point checklist before publishing

1. The main idea appears as text, not only inside an image.
2. Every informative image has contextual alt text.
3. Charts and figures include an equivalent explanation.
4. Videos have reviewed, synchronized captions.
5. Important visual information is explained in audio or text.
6. Contrast and size were checked in mobile view.
7. Color is not the only means of communicating a difference.
8. Compound hashtags use a capital letter for each word.
9. Emojis are few and do not substitute for information.
10. The link has context and leads to a functional page.
11. The post was reviewed without sound and without relying on the image.
12. One person is responsible for correcting reported barriers.

## How do you measure whether the process is working?

Do not reduce measurement to "how many posts have alt text." Combine workflow compliance with quality and response:

- percentage of informative images with a reviewed alternative;
- percentage of videos with corrected captions before publishing;
- errors found after publication and correction time;
- percentage of critical campaigns with an accessible landing page;
- barriers reported by format, channel, and vendor;
- results of periodic reviews with people with disabilities.

A 100% rate in the alt text field can coexist with useless descriptions. It is worth reviewing a monthly sample and asking whether a person understands the content, not just whether the field exists.

## Frequently asked questions

### What makes a post accessible?

That the information can be understood without depending on a single sense or format, through text alternatives, captions, visual description, contrast, plain language, and functional links.

### What should alt text say?

It should communicate the purpose and relevant information of the image in that context. It does not need to inventory every detail or repeat nearby text.

### Are automatic captions sufficient?

They should not be published without review. Correct names, terms, punctuation, synchronization, and sounds necessary for understanding.

### How are accessible hashtags written?

Use an initial capital letter on each word of a compound hashtag—for example #AccessibleMarketing—and limit the quantity.

### Does an accessible post guarantee that the entire platform is accessible?

No. The team controls its content, but not the entire interface. For critical information, also provide an accessible web page.
