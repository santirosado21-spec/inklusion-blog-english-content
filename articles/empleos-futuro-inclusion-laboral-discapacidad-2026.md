# Jobs of the Future and Workforce Inclusion: Hiring Is Not Enough If Talent Cannot Grow

**Direct answer:** jobs of the future can either expand workforce inclusion or widen the gap. A report by Fundación Randstad published on August 6, 2026, shows that in Spain, many people with disabilities have higher levels of education yet remain concentrated in positions below their qualification level or in roles with declining skill demands. For companies, hiring is no longer enough: development, mobility, and genuine access to training must all be measured.

The uncomfortable finding is not that talent is lacking. It is that the talent may already be inside the organization, perfectly findable in the directory, and still left out of the projects that build the next promotion.

> An inclusive hire opens the door. An inclusive career keeps that door from leading to a dead end.

## What did the study published in August 2026 find?

On August 6, [EL PAÍS published the findings of the Fundación Randstad report](https://elpais.com/economia/formacion/2026-08-06/los-empleos-del-futuro-ponen-a-prueba-la-inclusion-laboral-de-las-personas-con-discapacidad.html) *Are People with Disabilities Ready for the Labor Market of the Future?* The data correspond to Spain and must be read with that scope in mind; they are not a measurement of the Mexican labor market.

The research reports real progress: 82% of people with disabilities of working age have intermediate or higher education; higher education grew 53% over a decade; and employment increased 42%, equivalent to 166,000 more people in work.

However, a mismatch emerges. Thirty-two percent of employed people with disabilities hold higher education qualifications, yet only 23% hold high-skilled positions. At the other extreme, 7% have basic schooling and elementary occupations account for 22% of employment. Education and opportunity are not advancing at the same pace.

The report adds another warning: 67% of employment within this group is concentrated in occupations linked to stabilized or declining skill sets. At the same time, technical and professional occupations grew more than 70% over ten years. The picture is not fatalistic; it demonstrates that progress is possible, but does not happen on its own.

## Why does this signal matter now?

The [World Economic Forum, in its *Future of Jobs Report 2025*](https://www.weforum.org/publications/the-future-of-jobs-report-2025/digest/), estimated that 39% of workers' current skills will be transformed or become outdated between 2025 and 2030. The report gathered responses from more than 1,000 employers representing more than 14 million workers across 55 economies.

The same source calculated that, if the global workforce were 100 people, 59 would need training before 2030. It also identified skills gaps as the main obstacle to business transformation: 63% of surveyed employers identified them as a significant barrier.

**Fact:** those percentages describe the Forum's global survey, not an individual prediction for each company nor an exclusive measurement of disability. **Our interpretation:** if training is distributed with the same biases that already limit promotion, technological transformation can automate tasks and also freeze careers.

## What reading is appropriate for Mexico?

It is not advisable to import Spanish figures as if they were Mexican. It is advisable to take the pattern seriously: improving educational attainment and hiring is insufficient when assignment, learning, and promotion processes continue to leave barriers in place.

The [OECD documented in 2022](https://www.oecd.org/en/publications/disability-work-and-inclusion_1eaa5e9c-en.html) that, across 32 countries, the employment rate of people with disabilities was 27 percentage points lower than that of people without disabilities, and that the gap had persisted throughout the previous decade. The organization called for integrating disability into mainstream employment, skills, and social protection policies, rather than intervening too late.

For a company in Mexico, the practical question is straightforward: are people with disabilities present in the pathways where the business is learned? That includes critical projects, mentoring, technology training, client meetings, certifications, rotations, leadership, and succession processes.

Counting hires can show access. It does not by itself show whether professional development exists. A dashboard that celebrates new joiners and never reviews promotions captures only the opening scene.

## Where is the gap built inside a company?

### In project assignments

Emerging skills are learned in courses, but also by solving real problems. If a person is systematically kept in operational tasks "because they already work well there," they lose exposure to the tools, clients, and decisions that later appear as requirements for promoting them.

### In inaccessible platforms and materials

A learning license is not the same as access. Videos without subtitles, labs incompatible with keyboard or screen reader, evaluations with rigid time limits, and poorly structured documents turn training into a barrier. Inklusion's guide on [accessible training](https://inklusion-blog.vercel.app/blog/capacitacion-accesible-personas-discapacidad-checklist/) explains how to review the announcement, platform, materials, and evaluation.

### In inflated requirements

Some promotions demand capabilities that are not essential to the outcome: permanent physical availability, a single way of presenting, mobility without analyzing alternatives, or experience obtained only in projects to which the process itself restricts access.

### In the absence of adjustments

A change of role may require new tools, schedules, or support. The process of [reasonable adjustments at work](https://inklusion-blog.vercel.app/blog/ajustes-razonables-trabajo-discapacidad-mexico/) must accompany mobility and promotion, not appear only during recruitment.

## Five decisions to make the future of work inclusive

### 1. Measure career trajectory, not just representation

Review access to training, completion rates, internal mobility, promotions, retention, and time at level. Segment carefully by job family and level to avoid confusing structural differences with unequal treatment. Protect privacy and avoid reports that allow individuals to be identified.

### 2. Audit the learning experience

Test platforms, materials, sessions, and evaluations with different assistive technologies. Include reviewed subtitles, alternatives for visual content, keyboard navigation, and clear channels for requesting adjustments.

### 3. Open the projects that generate experience

Identify which assignments typically precede a promotion and who receives those opportunities. Publish the criteria. If selection always happens through informal recommendation, the company does not have a talent pathway; it has a network of acquaintances.

### 4. Separate essential function from habit

Before ruling someone out for an emerging role, define the expected outcome. Then analyze technology, adjustments, and different ways of achieving it. The useful question is not whether the person performs the task the way it has always been done, but whether they can meet the relevant standard.

### 5. Include accessibility when purchasing AI and software

New tools can enable autonomy or create another layer of exclusion. Evaluate compatibility with assistive technologies, keyboard workflows, documentation, support, and contractual remedies. Consult the [checklist for purchasing accessible software](https://inklusion-blog.vercel.app/blog/comprar-software-accesible-checklist-proveedores/) before signing.

## Is artificial intelligence a threat or an opportunity?

**Fact:** the World Economic Forum reports that AI and big data lead the fastest-growing skills, and that technology literacy is also gaining importance. **Hypothesis:** AI can expand opportunities for some people through communication, productivity, or access supports, but it can also exclude when interfaces are inaccessible, data reproduces biases, or training does not reach everyone.

There is no universal answer because "AI" is not a single role or a single tool. A responsible assessment must examine concrete tasks: which ones are changing, what skills the transition demands, who receives training, and what barriers the system presents.

Inklusion's position is practical: do not wait for the transformation to end before asking who was left out. When accessibility and development are designed from the start, correcting course stops being a race against the clock.

## Frequently asked questions

### What is the relationship between jobs of the future and workforce inclusion?

Technological transformation changes roles and skills. If people with disabilities remain concentrated in tasks with lower career prospects or are excluded from training, the company reproduces a gap even if hiring has improved.

### Does the Fundación Randstad study describe Mexico?

No. The published data correspond to Spain. They function as a signal and point of comparison, not as a Mexican measurement.

### What should a company measure beyond hires?

Access to training, promotions, mobility, retention, adjustments, accessibility of tools, and distribution by level and job family — always with privacy and context.

### What is accessible reskilling?

It is training designed to transition people toward new roles without barriers in platforms, materials, sessions, or evaluations, and with reasonable adjustments when needed.

### Does AI necessarily eliminate jobs held by people with disabilities?

This cannot be stated as a general rule. The effect depends on tasks, access to training, accessibility of tools, and organizational decisions.
