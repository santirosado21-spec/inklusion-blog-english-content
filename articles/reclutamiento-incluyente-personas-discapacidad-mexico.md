# Inclusive Recruitment of People with Disabilities: A Guide for Companies in Mexico

**Direct answer:** an inclusive recruitment process allows people with disabilities to learn about a job opening, apply, participate in interviews, and demonstrate their competencies without avoidable barriers. To achieve this, the company must review the job description, make its channels accessible, offer reasonable adjustments, prepare interviewers, and extend inclusion to onboarding, development, and retention.

Posting "we are an inclusive company" is easy. What is difficult—and what is valuable—is proving it when a person uses a screen reader, needs an interview in a space without steps, requests written instructions, or requires a different format for an assessment. That is where you can tell whether inclusion is a culture or merely copy.

> The goal is not to hire someone "in spite of" their disability. It is to evaluate talent without confusing barriers in the process with a lack of ability.

## What Inclusive Recruitment Is

It is a way of designing attraction, selection, and hiring so that more people can compete on fair terms. It does not eliminate the essential requirements of the role. It eliminates inherited requirements, inaccessible tests, rigid channels, and assumptions that do not predict performance.

The [Convention on the Rights of Persons with Disabilities, Article 27](https://www.un.org/development/desa/disabilities/convention-on-the-rights-of-persons-with-disabilities/article-27-work-and-employment.html), recognizes the right to work in an open, inclusive, and accessible labor market and work environment. It includes non-discrimination in recruitment, hiring, retention, and promotion, as well as the provision of reasonable adjustments.

In Mexico, the [General Law for the Inclusion of Persons with Disabilities](https://www.diputados.gob.mx/LeyesBiblio/pdf/LGIPD.pdf) establishes the national inclusion framework. For a company, the practical point is clear: opening a vacancy is not enough; you must review whether the process genuinely allows people to participate.

## Seven Barriers That Drive Away Talent Before the Interview

### 1. Inflated Job Descriptions

"Excellent presentation," "works well under pressure," a driver's license, willingness to travel, or the ability to lift heavy loads appear frequently even when they are not essential. Every unnecessary requirement reduces the candidate pool without improving selection. Separate indispensable functions from preferences and historical habits.

### 2. Job Postings Published in Inaccessible Formats

An image containing all the text, an unstructured PDF, or a portal that cannot be operated with a keyboard excludes people before Human Resources ever sees their profile. Publish information as HTML text as well, and review forms, labels, contrast, visible focus, and error messages. A [web accessibility audit](https://inklusion-blog.vercel.app/blog/auditoria-accesibilidad-web-wcag-checklist-mexico/) helps identify these barriers.

### 3. A Single Channel for Applying

If the form fails and there is no email address, phone number, or other route, the application dies for a technical reason. Offering an accessible alternative is not "giving an advantage"; it is preventing software from deciding who deserves to be evaluated.

### 4. Tests That Measure Format, Not Competency

A timed assessment, a noisy group exercise, or a platform incompatible with assistive technology may measure visual speed, sensory tolerance, or proficiency with an interface, even when the role requires something else entirely. Ask what ability each exercise is intended to measure and allow equivalent formats.

### 5. Unprepared Interviewers

Discomfort produces invasive questions, excessive assistance, or a conversation focused on the diagnosis. The team must talk about experience, competencies, essential functions, and the support needed to participate. Dignified treatment does not mean ignoring the disability; it means addressing it only when relevant and with respect.

### 6. Inaccessible Offices or Video Calls

Before inviting someone in, verify the entrance, elevator, restroom, signage, and route. For remote interviews, confirm that the platform supports keyboard navigation, captions, and assistive technology. If the organization receives candidates on site, the [universal design guide for offices](https://inklusion-blog.vercel.app/blog/diseno-universal-oficinas-comercios-checklist-mexico/) serves as a starting point.

### 7. Silence About Adjustments

Many people do not request an adjustment because they fear being screened out or do not know who to contact. Include a visible statement: "If you need any adjustment to participate in the process, let us know how we can support you." Then, of course, you must actually respond. A black-hole inbox with a nice phrase is still a black-hole inbox.

## How to Write an Accessible Job Posting

A good job posting answers clearly what the person will do, what results are expected, what requirements are essential, where the work takes place, how to apply, and how to request an adjustment.

- **Use a recognizable title:** avoid internal or creative names that no one searches for.
- **Describe outcomes:** "prepare the monthly inventory report" is more useful than "be a dynamic person."
- **Distinguish what is indispensable:** separate essential requirements from desirable skills.
- **Explain the working arrangement:** location, hours, remote or on-site work, and actual travel requirements.
- **Write in plain language:** short sentences, lists, and concrete terms.
- **Publish in HTML:** do not rely solely on images or scanned documents.
- **Include adjustments:** provide a clear contact for requesting them during the selection process.

Example of a useful closing statement: "We evaluate people on the competencies relevant to the role. If you need an adjustment to complete the application, interview, or assessment, write to us and let us know what format or support would allow you to participate."

## What Reasonable Adjustments Are and How to Manage Them

A reasonable adjustment is a necessary modification that allows a person to participate on equal terms. There is no magic catalogue: it is defined according to the person, the task, and the context. Two candidates with the same disability may require different types of support.

In recruitment, some examples include:

- sending instructions and key questions in writing;
- allowing the use of a screen reader, magnifier, or assistive device;
- enabling captions or interpretation where appropriate;
- converting a test to an accessible format that measures the same competency;
- providing additional time when the specific barrier justifies it;
- conducting the interview in a room with an accessible route;
- reducing stimuli or adjusting the sequence of the interview.

The internal process can be straightforward: receive the request, confirm with the person what they need, evaluate the solution quickly, assign a responsible party, deliver the adjustment, and verify whether it worked. Avoid requesting medical information by reflex. The useful question is: "What change would allow you to participate in this stage?"

## How to Interview Without Bias or Invasive Questions

Use a structured guide for all candidates. Ask comparable questions related to the essential functions, and score responses against criteria defined before the conversation. This improves consistency and reduces the temptation to decide based on "gut feelings."

- Ask about relevant experiences and results.
- Describe a specific function and ask how the candidate would carry it out.
- Do not turn the interview into a story of personal triumph over adversity.
- Address the person directly, not their companion or interpreter.
- Do not touch assistive devices, a wheelchair, a cane, or a service dog without permission.
- If you are unsure how to help, ask naturally and listen to the answer.

The interview should determine whether there is a match between competencies and the role. Diagnosing, guessing at personal risks, or congratulating someone for "their bravery" is not part of the Human Resources job description.

## Hiring Is the Beginning, Not the Goal

A process can be accessible all the way to the offer and then fail on the first day. Onboarding must include readable documents, compatible tools, a physical route, communication channels, team training, and follow-up on adjustments. It must also explain who to contact if a barrier arises later.

Sustainable workplace inclusion requires learning opportunities, fair evaluation, accessible feedback, and pathways for growth. If a person joins but never receives visible projects, training, or the possibility of advancement, the organization has achieved presence, not inclusion.

## What to Measure Without Turning People Into a Number

Measuring helps identify barriers, provided that confidentiality is protected and the purpose of the information is explained. A useful dashboard may include:

- percentage of job postings reviewed against accessibility criteria;
- attraction channels used and the accessibility of each;
- adjustment requests, response times, and adjustments delivered;
- progress by stage to identify drop-off points;
- candidate experience;
- retention, mobility, and access to training;
- recurring barriers and the date they were corrected.

Do not use the hiring of one person as a corporate trophy. Evaluate whether the system is changing: better job postings, more accessible tools, prepared leaders, and fewer barriers to participation.

## A 30-Day Plan to Get Started

1. **Days 1–5:** choose three frequently posted roles and remove requirements that do not correspond to essential functions.
2. **Days 6–10:** test the employment portal with a keyboard, a screen reader, and plain language; enable an alternative channel.
3. **Days 11–15:** define the workflow for requesting, approving, delivering, and documenting reasonable adjustments.
4. **Days 16–20:** train recruiters and leaders in structured interviewing, dignified treatment, and inclusive language.
5. **Days 21–25:** review the physical and digital accessibility of interviews, assessments, and onboarding.
6. **Days 26–30:** measure the experience, correct barriers, and assign responsible parties for the following quarter.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### What is inclusive recruitment?

It is an attraction, selection, and hiring process that removes unnecessary barriers and allows candidates to demonstrate the competencies relevant to the role, with reasonable adjustments when required.

### Should disability be asked about in an interview?

The conversation should focus on the functions of the role and the adjustments needed to participate, not on diagnoses or personal information unrelated to the work.

### What are reasonable adjustments in recruitment?

They are specific modifications such as accessible materials, an alternative test format, assistive technology, an accessible space, or time adjustments when there is a concrete need.

### How do you know whether a job posting is accessible?

It must be findable, understandable, and actionable without avoidable barriers; explain essential functions; allow alternative contact methods; and indicate how to request adjustments.

### What should a company measure after hiring?

Process accessibility, delivery of adjustments, candidate experience, retention, development, and recurring barriers, with confidentiality protections in place.

### Does workplace inclusion end when the contract is signed?

No. It continues with accessible onboarding, tools, leadership, fair evaluation, professional development, and follow-up on adjustments.

## Conclusion

Inclusive recruitment does not ask for charity or lower standards. It asks for better-designed processes. When a company distinguishes essential functions from unnecessary requirements, offers adjustments, prepares its team, and measures barriers, it broadens its access to talent and builds a more coherent culture.

The first step does not need to be a major campaign. It can be reviewing the next job posting before publishing it and asking: "Does this evaluate ability, or does it simply reward whoever best adapts to our process?" That question tends to be worth more than many speeches.
