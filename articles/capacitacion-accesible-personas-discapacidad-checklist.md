# How to Create Accessible Training for People with Disabilities: A Checklist for Companies

**Direct answer:** accessible training allows people with different disabilities to learn about the announcement, register, enter the space or platform, perceive the content, participate, practice, and demonstrate learning. To achieve this, the company must anticipate accessibility needs, offer a channel for requesting adjustments, prepare compatible materials, facilitate using more than one mode of communication, and evaluate outcomes without confusing a barrier with a lack of ability.

Accessibility does not begin when someone requests subtitles five minutes before the session. It begins when choosing the platform, the room, the format of the materials, and the method of evaluation. Anticipating common needs reduces improvisation; maintaining an adjustments process covers what the general design could not foresee.

## What does it mean for training to be accessible?

Accessible training removes barriers throughout the entire learning experience. This includes in-person events, virtual courses, hybrid sessions, onboarding, workshops, internal certifications, and recorded content.

The [Web Accessibility Initiative of the W3C](https://www.w3.org/WAI/teach-advocate/accessible-presentations/) recommends planning accessibility from the outset of meetings, conferences, trainings, and presentations. Its checklist covers facilities or platforms, materials, speakers, communication, supports, and follow-up.

There are two layers that must coexist:

- **Anticipated accessibility:** subtitles, clear structure, contrast, accessible digital materials, unobstructed routes, breaks, and multiple ways to participate—available to everyone.
- **Reasonable adjustments:** supports defined for an individual need, such as Mexican Sign Language interpretation, additional time, a change of location, an alternative format, or assistive technology.

Designing the first layer well reduces urgent requests. It does not eliminate the second. A general checklist will never know every person who will walk into the room.

## Before announcing: define the outcome, audience, and barriers

Start with the learning outcome. Should the person understand a policy, operate a tool, resolve a case, or demonstrate a technique? That answer makes it possible to distinguish the objective from the historical way of teaching it.

Then review foreseeable barriers:

- Does registration work with a keyboard and screen reader?
- Does the platform support subtitles, telephone access, and navigation without a mouse?
- Does the venue have an accessible route from the entrance, transportation or parking, as well as restrooms and signage?
- Do the slides, readings, videos, and exercises have accessible formats?
- Does the activity depend on seeing, hearing, speaking, writing by hand, or moving in a way that is not essential to learning?
- Is there time and budget for professional supports?

Do not assume what a person needs based on their disability. Preventive review corrects design barriers; individual conversation defines concrete supports.

## What should an accessible announcement say?

The announcement must provide the information needed to decide and participate: objective, agenda, duration, modality, location, requirements, activities, technology, and contact. If the event is in person, include access information. If it is virtual, identify the platform and any prior setup required.

Add a direct statement such as: "If you need any adjustment or accessible format to participate, please indicate this on this form or write to [contact]." Offer more than one channel and explain when the organization will respond.

A deadline helps with contracting interpretation or adapting materials, but it must not become a closed door. If a request arrives later, evaluate what can be implemented and communicate options honestly.

The announcement itself must also be accessible. An image with all the text embedded, published without an alternative, can exclude people before the course even begins. Also review [inclusive language on disability](https://inklusion-blog.vercel.app/blog/lenguaje-incluyente-discapacidad-guia-empresas-mexico/): invite people to request supports without requiring public diagnoses or using paternalistic terms.

## How to prepare accessible training materials

### Documents and slides

Use real titles and headings, a logical reading order, legible fonts, good contrast, descriptive links, and lists created with the program's built-in tools. Do not rely solely on color, position, or shape to communicate instructions. The WCAG 2.2 criteria on [use of color](https://www.w3.org/WAI/WCAG22/Understanding/use-of-color.html) and [sensory characteristics](https://www.w3.org/WAI/WCAG22/Understanding/sensory-characteristics.html) explain why "press the green button on the right" is not sufficient.

Describe images that convey information. For complex charts, include the conclusion and relevant data in text. Share materials before the session so each person can review them with their own technology and at their own pace.

If you deliver a PDF, do not convert it into a photograph of the slides. The [guide to creating accessible PDFs](https://inklusion-blog.vercel.app/blog/como-crear-pdf-accesible-checklist-empresas/) covers tagging, reading order, alternatives, and testing. When possible, also offer HTML or the accessible source document.

### Video and audio

Videos with spoken information require subtitles. The W3C explains that [subtitles must include dialogue and the sound information necessary](https://www.w3.org/WAI/media/av/captions/) to understand the content, such as who is speaking or a relevant sound.

Automatic subtitles can support a session, but they must be monitored. Names, figures, and technical terms are precisely where they tend to fail, and an error between "15" and "50" can change an entire instruction. For recorded content that will be reused, correct and synchronize the file.

When visual information is not explained in the audio, provide description. A facilitator can verbalize what they are pointing to on screen: "the blue line rises from 20 to 35 between April and June" communicates far more than "as you can see here."

### Platform and activities

Test the platform with a keyboard, zoom, and assistive technology. Check login, chat, raising a hand, polls, breakout rooms, files, and assessments. An access link that expires, an inaccessible CAPTCHA, or a drag-and-drop activity without an alternative can block participation.

Offer more than one way to respond when the mode is not part of the competency: voice, chat, an accessible form, or a document. If learning requires a specific technique, keep that requirement and provide the supports that allow it to be demonstrated.

## How to facilitate without creating new barriers

At the start, explain the agenda, breaks, tools, and how to request support privately. Identify who is speaking and read relevant chat questions aloud. Describe visual elements and allow time to respond.

Speaking clearly helps; speaking like an audiobook at double speed does not. Avoid filling every silence. Some people need more time to process, write, or use assistive technology.

In in-person sessions, keep routes clear and do not move assistive devices without permission. In hybrid modality, assign someone to manage chat and remote participation; asking the facilitator to manage two rooms, three platforms, and accessibility at the same time usually ends as a very good intention with eight tabs open.

If there is an interpreter, professional captioning, or personal support, speak to the participant, not to the support person. Share the agenda and technical vocabulary in advance. Support personnel also need to prepare.

## How to conduct an accessible evaluation

The evaluation must measure the outcome defined at the outset. If the objective is to make an ethical decision in a case study, a platform that requires dragging cards with a mouse adds an irrelevant skill. If the objective is to operate a machine, on the other hand, the mode of interaction may be essential and must be analyzed with appropriate safety measures and adjustments.

Review instructions, time, navigation, formats, feedback, and compatibility. Offer practice with the tool before using it for grading. When additional time or another adjustment is granted, protect confidentiality and avoid explaining it in front of the group.

Connect this process with the policy on [reasonable adjustments at work](https://inklusion-blog.vercel.app/blog/ajustes-razonables-trabajo-discapacidad-mexico/). Training should not invent a parallel channel every time a need arises.

## Accessible training checklist

### Planning

- The learning outcome is defined without confusing it with a format.
- There is a person responsible and a budget for accessibility and adjustments.
- The room or platform was tested, not merely declared accessible by the vendor.
- The agenda includes breaks and realistic participation times.

### Announcement and registration

- Information is in accessible text and not only in an image.
- The form works with a keyboard and assistive technologies.
- A confidential channel is offered for requesting adjustments.
- The organization confirms receipt, the responsible person, and the deadline.

### Content

- Documents and slides have structure, contrast, and a logical reading order.
- Informative images have alternatives and charts are explained.
- Videos have reviewed subtitles and description where needed.
- Materials are shared in advance and in usable formats.

### Session and evaluation

- The facilitator describes visual elements and identifies who is speaking.
- There is more than one way to participate when the mode is not essential.
- Agreed supports are ready from the start.
- The evaluation measures the target competency and allows for appropriate adjustments.
- There is a channel for reporting barriers during and after the session.

## What to measure after training

Do not only ask whether participants "liked" the course. Measure whether it worked:

- percentage of materials reviewed before publication;
- adjustment requests responded to and implemented on time;
- barriers reported by stage: registration, access, content, activity, and evaluation;
- dropouts or incomplete evaluations attributable to accessibility problems;
- percentage of recorded content with corrected subtitles;
- perceived autonomy, participation, and confidentiality;
- recurring changes that should be incorporated into the course template.

**Working hypothesis:** an accessible template and a prior review will reduce urgent requests and dropouts, but only data and the experience of participants can confirm this. The absence of complaints does not prove that no one encountered a barrier.

## Frequently asked questions

### What is accessible training?

It is an experience designed so that people with different disabilities can find out about it, enter, perceive content, participate, practice, and demonstrate learning without avoidable barriers.

### What should the company ask about adjustments?

What adjustment or support the person needs to participate. The conversation should focus on the barrier and the outcome, not on requesting unnecessary clinical information.

### Are automatic subtitles sufficient?

They can support a session, but they require monitoring. For reusable recordings, it is advisable to correct names, figures, terms, and synchronization.

### What formats should materials have?

They must have structure, contrast, alternatives, descriptive links, and a logical order. When possible, offer HTML or accessible source documents in addition to PDF.

### How do you evaluate without creating barriers?

Preserve the competency that must be demonstrated and change the format when it is not part of the objective. Offer adjustments and an accessible tool.

### How do you know whether training was accessible?

Review reported barriers, adjustments delivered, dropouts due to accessibility, compatibility of materials, and whether people were able to participate and demonstrate learning.

## Useful training begins before the first slide

Accessibility is not an add-on that the facilitator activates at the start. It is a chain of decisions: announcement, registration, platform, materials, activities, supports, and evaluation.

Start with a real course. Walk through each step using this checklist, correct three barriers before announcing, and publish an adjustments channel. The next course should not depend on rediscovering the same problems.
