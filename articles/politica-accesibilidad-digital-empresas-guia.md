# Digital Accessibility Policy: 10 Decisions to Keep It from Being Filed Away

**Direct answer:** a digital accessibility policy defines the scope, standard, responsible parties, controls, and metrics with which an organization removes barriers in websites, applications, documents, and content. For it to work, it must be embedded in briefs, procurement, acceptance criteria, and maintenance. If it only promises inclusion without assigning decisions, dates, or evidence, it is a statement of intent—not an operational policy.

The problem is rarely convincing the organization that accessibility "is important." The problem appears when a campaign arrives on a Friday, a new platform has already been contracted, and no one knows who could have stopped an inaccessible publication from going live.

> A policy is useful when it reduces ambiguity before the problem arises. After the problem, it should at least say who is accountable and how the organization learns from it.

## What should a digital accessibility policy resolve?

It must convert a broad commitment into repeatable decisions. The [W3C recommends](https://www.w3.org/WAI/planning/org-policies/) defining reference standards, conformance level, scope, milestones, third-party content, monitoring, and review.

That means a person should be able to read the policy and answer—without creative interpretation:

- which websites, apps, documents, campaigns, and internal tools are included;
- which technical and experience criteria apply;
- who approves requirements and who makes corrections;
- what happens with legacy or third-party technology;
- how testing is conducted and what evidence is retained;
- how a user reports a barrier.

## Are a policy, a statement, and an audit the same thing?

No. They serve different functions.

### Accessibility policy

It is the organization's operating rule. It can be internal and must connect with design, development, content, procurement, quality, human resources, and customer service.

### Accessibility statement

It is a public communication. The [W3C guide for accessibility statements](https://www.w3.org/WAI/planning/statements/) recommends including the commitment, the standard applied, and a contact for reporting problems; it also suggests disclosing known limitations, measures adopted, and tested environments.

### Accessibility audit

It is an evaluation at a specific point in time and within a defined scope. It provides findings and evidence, but it does not replace the policy. The Inklusion guide on [web accessibility audits](https://inklusion-blog.vercel.app/blog/auditoria-accesibilidad-web-wcag-checklist-mexico/) explains how to prioritize blockers and build a remediation plan.

## The 10 decisions a policy must contain

### 1. Name a sponsor and a responsible function

The sponsor removes blockers and allocates resources. The responsible function maintains the policy, coordinates evaluations, and reports progress. Neither one does all the work.

### 2. Define the scope

Include public products, intranet, applications, documents, emails, videos, recruitment systems, and third-party flows according to the organization's reality. A scope that says "all channels" but has no inventory is difficult to manage.

### 3. Choose a verifiable technical reference

Define the version and level the organization will use. WCAG 2.2 Level AA is a frequently used technical reference. Do not promise AAA as an aspirational gesture if there is no capacity to verify and maintain it.

**Legal note:** citing WCAG does not by itself determine all applicable obligations. Regulatory scope depends on country, sector, type of entity, and contract; it must be reviewed with competent legal counsel.

### 4. Assign tasks by role

Content writes headings and alternatives; design defines contrast, focus, and states; development implements semantics and operability; quality assurance tests; procurement demands evidence; customer service receives barrier reports. The [W3C ARRM draft](https://www.w3.org/WAI/planning/arrm/) proposes primary, secondary, and contributing responsibilities to prevent everything from falling to development at the end.

### 5. Integrate accessibility into the product lifecycle

Add requirements to the brief, user stories, design system, definition of done, acceptance testing, and change control. The policy must specify what evidence is required before release.

### 6. Include procurement and third parties

A checkout, a map, a job platform, or an external media player can block the entire user journey. Define requirements in the request, demonstration, contract, acceptance, and remediation plan. The [checklist for buying accessible software](https://inklusion-blog.vercel.app/blog/comprar-software-accesible-checklist-proveedores/) details how to review ACR/VPATs, testing, and vendor commitments.

### 7. Create a combined evaluation strategy

Combine automated testing, manual review, keyboard and assistive technology testing, and participation by people with disabilities. The [WCAG-EM 2 methodology](https://www.w3.org/WAI/test-evaluate/conformance/wcag-em/) structures the work into scope, exploration, sample, evaluation, and reporting.

### 8. Define how issues are prioritized and remediated

Classify by impact on tasks, severity, scope, and frequency. A form that prevents someone from making a purchase or submitting a job application must take priority over a cosmetic defect repeated on a rarely visited page.

### 9. Open an accessible feedback channel

Publish a contact that is easy to find and allow reporting in more than one format where feasible. Define acknowledgment time, responsible party, investigation, response, and closure. Do not ask users to translate their experience into a WCAG criterion.

### 10. Review the policy and its results

Establish a periodic review and define events that require an update: redesign, acquisition, new app, change of standard, or a serious barrier. The policy must be able to change without losing traceability.

## How to distribute responsibilities without creating more bureaucracy

Use a brief matrix by activity. There is no need to fill in forty columns; what is needed is a clear decision.

- **Leadership:** approves objectives, scope, and resources.
- **Product:** integrates accessibility into priorities and acceptance criteria.
- **Content and marketing:** produces accessible text, images, video, and documents.
- **Design and development:** incorporates accessible patterns and prevents regressions.
- **Quality assurance:** executes tests and retains evidence.
- **Procurement and legal:** incorporates requirements, remedies, and risk review.
- **Customer service:** receives reports without questioning the person's experience.
- **Specialist or consultancy:** defines criteria, provides guidance, and evaluates; does not replace the other roles.

The reality is that a policy without distributed ownership creates a bottleneck. A policy with distributed tasks but no central responsible party creates different versions of the same standard. Both layers are needed.

## What metrics show whether the policy is working?

Avoid a single "accessibility percentage." It can obscure what was evaluated, with what method, and which barriers prevent critical tasks.

- percentage of critical user journeys evaluated in the period;
- critical and high barriers open, remediated, and overdue;
- median time from report to remediation;
- recurrence rate by component or template;
- digital purchases with requirements and acceptance testing;
- publications and documents reviewed before release;
- user reports with acknowledgment, response, and closure;
- participation of people with disabilities in research and testing.

**Interpretation:** a drop in reports does not always mean improvement; it may also indicate that the channel is hard to find or does not inspire confidence. Cross support data with independent evaluation and experience testing.

## Brief template for drafting the first version

You can structure the document as follows:

1. **Purpose:** what barriers it seeks to prevent and for whom.
2. **Scope:** products, content, locations, and third parties covered.
3. **Reference:** standard, version, level, and future transition.
4. **Governance:** sponsor, responsible function, and tasks by role.
5. **Lifecycle:** requirements from planning through maintenance.
6. **Evaluation:** methods, frequency, sample, and evidence.
7. **Remediation:** severity, timelines, and documented exceptions.
8. **Feedback:** channels, timelines, and escalation.
9. **Measurement:** indicators and reporting.
10. **Review:** date, responsible party, and triggers for updates.

Then validate the policy against a real project. If it does not help make a decision about an image, a purchase, a launch date, or a reported barrier, it is still too far removed from operations.

## Four mistakes that make a policy useless

### Promising without defining limits

"Everything will be accessible" does not indicate what will be reviewed first or how legacy technology will be handled.

### Delegating everything to a single team

Accessibility cannot be fixed through code alone when the problem originated in content, procurement, or product definition.

### Using a scan as a certificate

Automation helps scale, but it does not test all criteria or the full user experience. The tool delivers data; the organization retains responsibility.

### Concealing known limitations

Documenting them does not replace remediation, but it allows the organization to communicate alternatives, prioritize, and be accountable. Pretending perfection tends to last only until someone tries to complete the task.

## Frequently asked questions

### What is a digital accessibility policy?

An agreement that defines scope, standard, responsible parties, controls, remediation, and metrics for digital products and content.

### Is it the same as an accessibility statement?

No. The policy governs operations; the statement publicly communicates commitments, current status, limitations, and a contact.

### What standard should it cite?

A specific one that the organization can apply and verify. WCAG 2.2 AA is a frequently used reference; the legal obligation must be reviewed according to context.

### Who should be responsible?

There must be a function that governs the system, with tasks distributed across product, content, design, development, procurement, quality assurance, and customer service.

### How is it measured to see if it works?

Through journey coverage, critical barriers, remediation timelines, recurrence, evaluated purchases, and user experience.
