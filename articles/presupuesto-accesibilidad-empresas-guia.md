# Accessibility Budget: How to Fund Remediation, Prevention, and Continuous Improvement

**Direct answer:** an accessibility budget must fund diagnosis, priority remediation, prevention, training, accessible procurement, testing with people with disabilities, and ongoing monitoring. There is no universal percentage: the amount depends on each organization's inventory, barriers, and risk. The useful decision is to allocate resources to verifiable outcomes, with clear owners and deadlines—not to a generic "inclusion" pool.

The budget usually arrives too late. A platform is purchased, a space is renovated, or a service is launched first; then someone discovers that fixing it requires a contract, development work, and additional time. Accessibility then appears expensive, even though much of that cost comes from having postponed it.

> Budgeting for accessibility is not about guessing an annual figure. It is about deciding which barriers will not be funded again.

## What should an accessibility budget cover?

The [W3C Web Accessibility Initiative](https://www.w3.org/WAI/planning-and-managing/) includes "determining budget and resources" within the planning phase, alongside policy, responsibilities, environment review, and monitoring. The guide also separates initiating, planning, implementing, and sustaining. That structure helps avoid a budget line that only pays for audits and never funds the actual remediation.

For a company, the scope can include digital products, documents, communications, employment, customer service, facilities, and events. Not every front requires the same level of investment at the same time. They do all require a shared inventory and an explicit criterion for prioritization.

**Fact:** the W3C recommends integrating accessibility throughout the web production process and repeating activities to continuously build capacity. **Inklusion's interpretation:** if the budget covers only a one-time remediation project but does not change design, procurement, and quality control practices, the debt will grow again.

## Seven budget lines that prevent costs from being overlooked

### 1. Diagnosis and baseline

Fund inventory, expert evaluation, automated testing as a support tool, and review of critical tasks. Define which assets will be evaluated: websites, applications, documents, kiosks, internal portals, talent processes, or physical spaces. An audit without a clear scope produces a count of findings, not a useful baseline.

### 2. Remediation and verification

Separate the cost of detecting from the cost of fixing. Include design, content, development, QA, vendor management, and retesting. For the digital front, our [90-day web accessibility guide](https://inklusion-blog.vercel.app/blog/plan-accesibilidad-web-90-dias-metricas-responsables/) explains how to move findings into production with owners and evidence.

### 3. Prevention in products and content

Reserve resources for design systems, accessible components, templates, acceptance criteria, editorial review, and well-applied automation. Prevention does not eliminate all testing, but it reduces the cost of repeating the same fix across twenty pages.

### 4. Role-specific training

A general awareness course can build awareness; it rarely changes day-to-day work on its own. Budget separate training for procurement, design, development, content, human resources, facilities, customer service, and leadership. The [W3C's Accessibility Roles and Responsibilities Mapping](https://www.w3.org/WAI/planning/arrm/) is useful for mapping WCAG tasks to specific roles.

### 5. Participation and testing with people with disabilities

Include recruitment, compensation, accommodations, facilitation, and time to integrate findings. The W3C explains why [involving users improves accessibility projects](https://www.w3.org/WAI/planning/involving-users/). This does not replace a technical conformance evaluation; it provides evidence about real-world use. Inklusion's guide on [testing with people with disabilities](https://inklusion-blog.vercel.app/blog/pruebas-accesibilidad-con-usuarios-discapacidad-guia/) details how to organize sessions without turning them into an exam.

### 6. Procurement and contracts

Account for time spent on requirements, accessible demonstrations, ACR or VPAT review where applicable, acceptance testing, and contractual remediation. Buying first and negotiating access afterward reduces options. Our [checklist for buying accessible software](https://inklusion-blog.vercel.app/blog/comprar-software-accesible-checklist-proveedores/) helps turn this budget line into verifiable decisions.

### 7. Operations, adjustments, and contingency

Reserve capacity for urgent barriers, reasonable accommodations, alternative formats, support, and technology changes. A contingency fund should not replace prevention. It serves to respond when an unforeseen need arises without requiring the person to wait until the next budget cycle.

## What to fund first when there is not enough money for everything?

Start with essential tasks and serious consequences. A barrier that prevents someone from applying for a job, making a payment, authenticating an account, receiving care, evacuating, or using a work tool must take priority over a cosmetic improvement.

Next, consider scope and recurrence. Fixing a shared component can resolve a barrier across many screens; changing a template can prevent hundreds of new inaccessible documents. Priority does not depend solely on counting errors, but on understanding which task is being blocked and how many times the root cause is reproduced.

Finally, combine remediation with prevention. If the entire budget goes toward historical debt, the team will keep creating barriers. If everything is dedicated to future processes, the people who are blocked today will keep waiting. A sensible portfolio funds both.

## A decision model that avoids inventing percentages

There is no percentage of revenue, technology spend, or headcount that works for every company. Publishing one without knowing an organization's assets, contracts, maturity, barriers, and exposure would be fictional precision.

Instead, build three scenarios:

**Minimum operating scenario:** eliminate critical blockers, enable a response channel, assign owners, and establish criteria to avoid introducing new priority barriers.

**Debt-reduction scenario:** add phased remediation, shared components, role-specific training, user testing, and supplier requirements.

**Sustainable-capacity scenario:** embed accessibility into planning, design, procurement, development, facilities, talent, monitoring, and executive review.

Each scenario should show which assets are covered, what the deliverables are, which third-party dependencies exist, what risk remains, and what the expected outcome is. That way, leadership consciously chooses what to fund and what risk to accept—rather than receiving a mysterious figure with a friendly label.

## Where should the budget live?

A central budget line can fund governance, standards, consulting, cross-cutting evaluations, and shared tools. However, each area must bear the accessibility cost of what it controls. Technology funds the product; marketing funds content and campaigns; human resources funds processes and accommodations; procurement funds vendor evaluation; facilities funds the physical environment.

Centralizing all costs in the inclusion team creates two problems. First, it hides how much each product actually costs. Second, it turns accessibility into an optional service that other areas request only when they remember to. The central team should facilitate and oversee; it should not carry the bill for other teams' decisions alone.

## How to know whether the investment produced access and not just activity?

Avoid measuring success by courses delivered, tools purchased, or audits completed. Those are inputs. The important evidence lies in observable change.

Review: critical barriers that have been remediated and verified; the percentage of essential tasks that can be completed; resolution time; recurrence by component or vendor; coverage of accessibility criteria in procurement; experience reported by users and employees; and findings that reach production.

The [W3C's business case for digital accessibility](https://www.w3.org/WAI/business-case/) recommends tailoring the argument to each organization's objectives and risks. That same logic applies to measurement: an e-commerce company, an employer, and an educational institution should not be presenting the same dashboard.

**Hypothesis to test:** when teams pay for accessibility within their own projects and a central capability exists to verify it, recurrence and late-stage remediation decrease. Confirm this by comparing launches, timelines, and root causes across several cycles; do not treat it as a universal truth before measuring.

## Three mistakes that make costs appear unpredictable

**Paying for diagnosis without reserving budget for remediation.** The report arrives, gets classified, and waits. The organization knows its debt better, but people remain blocked.

**Buying a tool as a substitute for a process.** Automation, overlays, or validators can support specific tasks; they do not replace judgment, manual testing, user participation, or remediation of code and content.

**Treating every barrier as a one-off exception.** If the same error appears in every form, document, or procurement, the budget must address the shared root cause. Funding individual tickets one by one is an expensive way to avoid a systemic decision.

## Frequently asked questions

### What does an accessibility budget include?

Diagnosis, remediation, prevention, training, testing, accessible procurement, accommodations, monitoring, and contingency.

### Is there a universal percentage?

No. The amount depends on scope, existing debt, risk, channels, vendors, and internal capacity.

### What is funded first?

Barriers that block essential tasks or affect safety, employment, purchasing, payment, authentication, and customer service.

### Should there be a single budget line?

There can be central resources, but each area must fund the accessibility of the products and processes it controls.

### How is outcome demonstrated?

Through barriers that have been remediated and verified, completable tasks, lower recurrence, response times, and user experience.
