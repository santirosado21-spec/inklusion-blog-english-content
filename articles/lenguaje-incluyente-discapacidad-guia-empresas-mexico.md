# Inclusive Language on Disability: A Guide for Companies in Mexico

**Direct answer:** to speak about disability in an inclusive way, a company must place the person and their rights at the center, use straightforward terms such as "people with disabilities," respect how each person or community identifies, avoid euphemisms and narratives of pity or heroism, and describe concrete barriers rather than presenting disability as an individual problem. This standard should be applied in job postings, campaigns, press releases, social media, customer service, internal communications, and images.

Correcting a word does not fix a culture. It does reveal whether the organization is willing to examine how it sees, represents, and listens to people. The goal is not to communicate with fear or to run every email through a twenty-person committee. It is to communicate with precision, respect, and participation.

> A useful rule: if the disability adds no context, do not turn it into a label; if it does add context, do not hide it behind a euphemism.

## What Inclusive Disability Language Is

It is a form of communication that recognizes people with disabilities as rights-holders, avoids prejudice, and presents human diversity without condescension. It is not an endless list of forbidden words. It is a criterion for deciding what information is relevant, who has a voice, and what effect the message produces.

The [UN Geneva guidelines on disability-inclusive language](https://www.ungeneva.org/en/about/accessibility/disability-inclusive-language) explain that words influence perception and can become barriers to participation. They recommend person-centered language as a starting point, respect for self-identification, the absence of labels and stereotypes, and avoiding condescending euphemisms.

In Mexico, the [General Law for the Inclusion of Persons with Disabilities](https://www.diputados.gob.mx/LeyesBiblio/pdf/LGIPD.pdf) establishes a framework based on equality, accessibility, participation, and inclusion. A corporate style guide does not replace legal advice, but it can translate those principles into everyday communication decisions.

## What Terms to Use and Why

### People with Disabilities

This is the recommended term for general institutional communication and the one used by the Convention on the Rights of Persons with Disabilities. It places the person first and names the disability without disguising it.

That does not mean there is a single formula for every context. Some individuals and communities prefer forms tied to their identity, such as "Deaf person," "blind person," or "autistic person." If you are speaking about a specific individual, ask how they prefer to identify. If you are communicating to a broad group, use an accepted term, explain the context, and avoid assuming the population is homogeneous.

### Barriers, Accessibility, and Supports

Describe what happens in the environment. "The form cannot be operated with a keyboard" conveys more than "the user cannot fill out the form." "The room lacks an accessible route" enables action; "the person has a mobility problem" shifts the responsibility.

Where relevant, refer to reasonable adjustments, accessible formats, assistive technology, interpretation, subtitles, easy-read materials, or Mexican Sign Language. Precision prevents "special help" from becoming a catch-all where everything fits and no one knows who is responsible for what.

## Expressions to Avoid

- **"Different abilities" or "special abilities":** these are imprecise euphemisms. All people have different abilities; the phrase neither explains the disability nor the barrier.
- **"Special needs":** this can sound condescending and obscures a concrete need. It is better to name the specific support or adjustment required.
- **"Handicapped," "invalid," or language that treats disability as the whole person:** these terms can be outdated, demeaning, or reductive. In general communication, "person with a disability" is widely used. Some people and communities prefer identity-first language such as "disabled person"; follow their stated preference when known.
- **"Suffers from," "afflicted by," or "confined to a wheelchair":** these presuppose suffering or confinement. A wheelchair is a mobility tool; it can provide autonomy.
- **"Normal" versus "person with a disability":** this turns one group into the measure of what is acceptable. When the distinction is necessary, "person without a disability" is usually more precise.
- **"An inspiration" for carrying out ordinary activities:** this turns a person's life into motivation for others. Acknowledge achievements on their own merits and in context, not out of surprise at the disability.
- **Using diagnoses as insults or metaphors:** expressions such as "how blind," "that's crazy," or "the idea was born lame" normalize negative associations, even when no person with a disability is present.

Language changes with use and with communities. A guide should not become a grammar police force or an excuse to speak about people without them. It should be reviewed, preferences should be heard, and choices that do not work should be corrected naturally.

## Examples: From a Problematic Phrase to Useful Communication

### In a Job Posting

**Avoid:** "We are looking for people with different abilities who want to prove that there are no limits."

**Better:** "We evaluate the competencies relevant to the role. If you require an adjustment to participate in the application, interview, or assessment, please let us know what support or format you need."

The second version does not turn the candidate into a symbol, and it does explain an action. The [inclusive recruitment guide](https://inklusion-blog.vercel.app/blog/reclutamiento-incluyente-personas-discapacidad-mexico/) explains how to apply this standard throughout the entire process.

### In an Internal News Item

**Avoid:** "Despite being in a wheelchair, Ana managed to lead the project."

**Better:** "Ana led the project and coordinated delivery with the product and operations teams." If accessibility is relevant to the story and Ana agrees to share it, the specific barrier and the solution can be explained without presenting her mobility as a personal obstacle.

### In Customer Service

**Avoid:** "We have special staff to handle these cases."

**Better:** "What format or support would allow you to complete the process? I can resolve it here or contact the person responsible and follow up with you." The [customer service protocol for people with disabilities](https://inklusion-blog.vercel.app/blog/atencion-clientes-personas-discapacidad-protocolo-mexico/) helps turn the message into a process.

### In a Campaign

**Avoid:** a photograph of a person with a disability accompanied only by "Nothing is impossible."

**Better:** show people with disabilities as customers, professionals, students, or community members, with a message related to the product, service, or right the campaign communicates. Representation is not decoration.

## How to Tell Stories Without Pity or Heroism

The most common narratives tend to fall into two extremes: the tragedy that asks for compassion and the hero who "overcomes" their disability. Both reduce the person and leave out decisions, work, context, and barriers.

Before publishing a testimonial, case study, or photograph:

1. **Define the purpose.** Is the disability relevant to understanding the story, or is it only there to produce an emotional response?
2. **Obtain informed consent.** Explain where the content will appear, for how long, and with what data.
3. **Preserve the person's voice.** Do not rewrite their experience until it becomes the message the brand wanted from the start.
4. **Describe context and barriers.** Show what changed in the environment, the process, or the policy.
5. **Avoid disproportionate praise.** Working, studying, traveling, or shopping does not automatically make someone an inspiration.
6. **Acknowledge agency.** Include decisions, skills, opinions, and outcomes.

A simple test: remove the reference to disability and check whether a story still exists—one with information, conflict, decision, and outcome. If everything disappears, the person was probably functioning as an emotional resource.

## Language Is Also in Images, Audio, and Design

A piece can use the right terms and still exclude. Review who appears, what role they occupy, and how voice is distributed. If people with disabilities only illustrate donation campaigns but never products, employment, leadership, or everyday life, the representation remains narrow.

- Include alternative text that communicates the function of the image, not a mechanical list of traits.
- Add accurate subtitles to videos and a transcript when the content requires it.
- Do not place essential information exclusively inside an image.
- Use sufficient contrast, heading hierarchy, and descriptive links.
- Identify people using the same standard you would apply to any participant; do not mention disability by reflex.
- Avoid photographs that infantilize, invade privacy, or present assistance without autonomy.

To review digital channels, consult the [WCAG AA web accessibility guide](https://inklusion-blog.vercel.app/blog/accesibilidad-web-wcag-aa-mexico/). Respectful language and accessible format are parts of the same message; one does not compensate for the absence of the other.

## A Review Workflow for Communications Teams

1. **Inventory high-impact pieces:** website, campaigns, job postings, manuals, service formats, presentations, and social media templates.
2. **Flag risks:** labels, euphemisms, unnecessary diagnoses, metaphors, pity, heroism, lack of consent, and absence of accessibility.
3. **Verify facts and preferences:** confirm names, terms, quotes, supports, and how each participant wishes to be identified.
4. **Review the full representation:** text, image, audio, video, calls to action, and publication context.
5. **Test the formats:** keyboard, screen reader, zoom, subtitles, contrast, alternative text, and message comprehension.
6. **Include participation:** compensate specialized review or testing with people with disabilities where appropriate. Consulting is not asking for free labor.
7. **Approve with a clear owner:** avoid a situation where no one can make a correction for fear of triggering an endless authorization chain.
8. **Document and update:** record decisions, approved examples, and lessons learned so the next piece starts from a better position.

## What an Internal Inclusive Language Guide Should Contain

- brief principles grounded in rights, autonomy, and participation;
- recommended terms with context, not just a list of replacements;
- preferences that must be asked of specific individuals;
- examples for job postings, social media, press, customer service, events, and internal communications;
- criteria for testimonials, photographs, and consent;
- minimum accessibility requirements by format;
- a mechanism for questions, corrections, and updates;
- responsible parties and a review date.

The guide must fit into real work. A short version for daily reference and a longer one with criteria and cases usually works better than an eighty-page PDF that everyone promises to read "on Friday."

## What to Measure Without Rewarding Only the Absence of Complaints

- percentage of priority pieces reviewed;
- participation of people with disabilities in creation and validation;
- recurring errors by channel or team;
- correction time after a report;
- videos with subtitles and pieces with useful alternative text;
- accessible formats available;
- comprehension and trust reported by audiences;
- trained teams and observed application in real pieces.

The absence of complaints does not prove that communication is inclusive. It may also mean the audience has stopped expecting a response. Combine metrics with piece reviews, accessibility testing, and direct listening.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### What is the recommended term for talking about disability?

In institutional communication, "people with disabilities" is a clear starting point aligned with the rights-based approach. When speaking with a specific person or community, it is important to respect the way they choose to identify.

### Should you say "people with different abilities"?

It is not recommended. It is an imprecise euphemism that avoids naming the disability. "People with disabilities" is direct, respectful, and widely accepted.

### Can disability be mentioned in a campaign?

Yes, when it is relevant and done with precision, consent, and context. It should not be used as emotional decoration, nor should it be hidden when it helps explain barriers, supports, or participation.

### Is it better to say "Deaf person" or "person with a hearing disability"?

Both expressions may be appropriate depending on the identity and preference of the person or community. If referring to a specific individual, ask and respect their choice.

### Does inclusive language make a communication accessible?

Not on its own. Accessible formats, alternative text, subtitles, contrast, clear structure, interpretation, or Mexican Sign Language are also needed where appropriate, as well as usable channels.

### How can a company review its language?

It can inventory high-impact pieces, identify labels and stereotypes, validate preferences, review images and formats, involve people with disabilities, and establish a brief guide with designated owners.

## Conclusion

Inclusive language does not seek to hide disability or make every conversation fragile. It seeks to name people with respect, explain barriers with precision, and leave behind narratives that reduce whole lives to pity, diagnosis, or inspiration.

Start with three pieces your organization uses every week: a job posting, a social media post, and a customer service response. Ask who has a voice, what stereotype appears, and what concrete action the message offers. Replacing "special" with a more precise word helps; changing the process behind it helps far more.
