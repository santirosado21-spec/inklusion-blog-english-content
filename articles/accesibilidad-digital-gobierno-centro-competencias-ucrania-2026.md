# Ukraine Centralizes Public Digital Accessibility: The Lesson Is Not to Copy a Portal, but to Build Capacity

**Direct answer:** on August 7, 2026, Ukraine unveiled a central resource to help its public agencies create accessible websites, applications, and documents. The case matters because it brings together requirements, training, tools, and audits into a single institutional capacity. For governments and businesses in Mexico, the lesson is not to copy Ukrainian regulation: it is to stop treating accessibility as an isolated fix.

Publishing a standard is relatively straightforward. Getting the person who writes copy, designs interfaces, procures technology, or develops a public-service process to know what to do on Monday morning is another matter entirely.

> Digital accessibility advances when it stops depending on the specialist's memory and starts forming part of the working system.

## What did Ukraine announce on August 7, 2026?

The [United Nations Development Programme (UNDP) in Ukraine reported](https://www.undp.org/ukraine/press-releases/digital-accessibility-competence-centre-launches-information-resource-government-agencies) that the Digital Accessibility Competence Centre, attached to the state-owned enterprise Diia, launched a new web accessibility site. The resource was created within the DIA Support project with funding from the Government of Sweden.

The portal brings together requirements from Ukrainian legislation for public websites, development checklists, educational and analytical materials, and evaluation tools. The Centre also conducts audits of government websites, mobile applications, and electronic documents.

The date, the institutions, and the scope are significant: this is a Ukrainian public-sector initiative. The resolution cited by UNDP and the national standard based on WCAG 2.1 do not create obligations for a Mexican company.

## Why does a competence centre change the conversation?

A library can answer "what does the guide say?" A competence centre must answer harder questions: who applies the criterion? how is a procurement reviewed? what evidence is retained? who helps when the team encounters a barrier? how is the next version prevented from reintroducing it?

UNDP explains that the Centre was established in 2024 and advises authorities on implementation. It also reports that, since 2021, more than 5,000 representatives of state and local authorities have received training on accessible websites and content. In 2026, a first cohort of specialists for public agencies was trained.

**Fact:** Ukraine combined a central reference, training, advisory support, and auditing. **Our interpretation:** that combination is worth more than any standalone checklist, because it converts technical knowledge into a function that can be sustained and replicated.

## What does the new resource do well?

### It translates the standard into tasks

The [Competence Centre's website](https://accessibility.diia.gov.ua/index.html) explains accessibility through concrete situations: reading and understanding content, operating controls and forms, and using products with assistive technologies. It then moves down to requirements and resources for those who create the products.

### It dismantles two common shortcuts

The portal notes that an "accessible mode" toggle does not make a product accessible on its own. Semantics, structure, operation, and compatibility with assistive technologies must exist in the primary experience.

It also clarifies that automated testing does not catch everything. Automated tools can verify that an attribute exists, but cannot always determine whether alternative text is correct or whether the reading order makes sense. For this reason, manual review with a keyboard and screen reader is recommended.

### It connects learning with evaluation

Training without review can end in good intentions. Auditing without knowledge transfer can create dependency. By offering both, the model attempts to close that uncomfortable gap between "we already took the course" and "the service works."

## What can an organization in Mexico learn?

It is not advisable to import an institutional model without context. Ukraine is digitalizing public services during a war and operates under its own regulations. Mexico has a different legal, administrative, and business structure.

The transferable lesson is one of management: when many teams are creating digital experiences, someone must maintain criteria, resolve questions, and measure results. That function can live in a formal centre, in a design team, in technology, in customer experience, or in a cross-functional working group. The name matters less than the authority and the continuity.

For a Mexican organization, a minimum viable version can begin with five components:

- an inventory of critical digital services and user journeys;
- one accountable person and designated contacts in content, design, development, procurement, and customer service;
- documented technical and experience criteria;
- a channel for requesting support and reporting barriers;
- a calendar for evaluation, remediation, and follow-up.

## What mistakes should an accessibility "centre" avoid?

### Becoming the final checkpoint

If the specialist team receives a product two days before launch, it will find flaws at a point when fixing them already costs more. Their work must begin at the brief, the acceptance criteria, and the procurement stage—not only at the final sign-off.

### Confusing compliance with experience

WCAG provides verifiable criteria; people show whether a flow can be understood and completed. Both levels are necessary. The [WCAG-EM 2 methodology, published by W3C on July 23, 2026](https://www.w3.org/WAI/test-evaluate/conformance/wcag-em/), expanded its scope beyond web pages to applications and other digital products, and recommends involving people with disabilities in the evaluation.

### Measuring activity instead of outcomes

Number of courses, attendees, or scans are operational data. Outcome metrics look at open critical barriers, remediation time, evaluated journeys, recurrence, and experience reported by users.

## The position: accessibility needs organizational infrastructure

Ukraine's announcement does not prove that all its services are already accessible. It does show a useful decision: recognizing that the standard requires translation, people, tools, support, and measurement.

**Hypothesis:** organizations that centralize learning and distribute responsibility will correct the same error fewer times. The hypothesis must be tested with recurrence data, resolution times, and user testing; a new organizational chart, on its own, does not remove barriers.

Digital accessibility in government and business matures when the question stops being "who can review this?" and shifts to "what part of the process allowed this barrier to get this far?" That is where sustainable improvement begins.

## Frequently asked questions

### What did Ukraine launch on August 7, 2026?

A central resource site from the Digital Accessibility Competence Centre for public agencies and teams that create digital products.

### Does the Ukrainian model create obligations for Mexico?

No. Its regulation applies to Ukraine. The case serves as a management reference, not as a Mexican legal source.

### Why is publishing a guide not enough?

Because accountable owners, training, procurement criteria, evaluation, support, and follow-up are all needed to apply the guide consistently.

### Do automated tools verify all accessibility?

No. They detect a portion of issues; manual review and testing with people remain necessary.

### How can an organization get started?

By prioritizing critical services, assigning accountable owners, defining criteria, training roles, and evaluating complete user journeys with documented evidence.
