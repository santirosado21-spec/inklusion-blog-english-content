# Web Accessibility WCAG AA in Mexico: A Guide for Businesses and Institutions

In Mexico, web accessibility is still often treated as a technical afterthought: something reviewed at the end of a project, once the site has already been designed, developed, and published. That approach almost always proves costly. Fixing accessibility at the end means rebuilding components, rewriting content, redesigning flows, and documenting decisions that should have existed from the start.

The better approach is different: turn accessibility into a continuous process of diagnosis, prioritization, correction, and evidence. That way, accessibility stops depending on "goodwill" and becomes an operational capability of the organization.

> The question is not whether your site "looks good." The question is whether a person with a visual, auditory, motor, or cognitive disability—or an older adult—can use it independently.

## What It Means to Comply with WCAG AA

WCAG stands for Web Content Accessibility Guidelines, the most widely used international standard for evaluating digital accessibility. Level AA is the most common benchmark for organizations that need a serious balance between rigor, feasibility, and impact.

WCAG is organized around four principles: content must be perceivable, operable, understandable, and robust. In practice, this means the site must be readable with assistive technologies, navigable by keyboard, clearly understandable, and compatible with browsers and screen readers.

## The Most Common Errors on Corporate Websites

Most problems are not spectacular. They are everyday details that block important tasks:

- Buttons or links distinguished only by color.
- Text with low contrast against light backgrounds or images.
- Menus, forms, or carousels that do not work well with a keyboard.
- Relevant images without useful alternative text.
- Forms that do not explain errors clearly.
- Disorganized heading hierarchies that make screen-reader navigation difficult.
- PDFs or downloadable documents that are not accessible.

These findings affect people with disabilities, but also users on mobile devices, slow connections, older adults, people with temporary injuries, and customers who simply want to complete a task quickly.

## How to Conduct a Serious Diagnosis

An accessibility diagnosis should not be limited to running an automated tool. Tools help, but they do not catch everything. A complete review combines automation, manual inspection, and usability testing with assistive technologies.

1. **Automated review:** identifies contrast issues, missing labels, HTML structure, and obvious errors.
2. **Manual review:** evaluates visible focus, tab order, alternative texts, interactive states, and content clarity.
3. **Keyboard testing:** confirms that key tasks can be completed without a mouse.
4. **Screen-reader testing:** validates accessible names, landmarks, headings, and error messages.
5. **Impact-based prioritization:** separates real blockers from cosmetic improvements.

## What to Fix First

For a business or institution, not all findings carry the same weight. The priority should be to fix the points that block critical actions: contacting, purchasing, registering, searching for information, downloading documents, completing forms, or understanding requirements.

A simple matrix works well: severity of the problem, number of users affected, importance of the flow, and difficulty of the fix. This allows progress without paralyzing the team.

## Why It Is Also a Business Decision

Being accessible broadens the potential audience, improves the browsing experience, and can strengthen SEO positioning. A well-structured, semantic, and clear site is easier to understand for people, search engines, and assistive systems alike.

It also reduces reputational risk. A growing number of public, financial, educational, and corporate organizations need to demonstrate social responsibility and inclusion with evidence, not just institutional messaging.

## How to Turn It into Continuous Improvement

Accessibility does not end with a report. What matters is establishing a cycle:

- Audit main templates and components.
- Fix high-severity blockers.
- Document accessible patterns for design and development.
- Train content, marketing, product, and technology teams.
- Re-evaluate when new pages or campaigns are published.

This cycle prevents the site from degrading again with every redesign, landing page, or new form.

## Quick Checklist to Get Started

- Can the entire site be navigated by keyboard?
- Do all links and buttons have clear names?
- Does the text contrast meet minimum standards?
- Do forms explain errors and required fields?
- Do important images have useful alternative text?
- Does the heading structure help users understand the page?
- Are downloadable documents also accessible?
- Is there evidence/documentation of the corrections made?

## Conclusion

Web accessibility is not a visual layer or an isolated certification. It is a way of designing digital services so that more people can use them independently. For organizations that want to be more inclusive, the first step is clear: diagnose the real barriers, fix them in order of priority, and build a process that keeps accessibility alive.
