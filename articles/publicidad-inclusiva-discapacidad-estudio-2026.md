# Inclusive Advertising and Disability: Representation Works When It Shows Agency, Not Vulnerability

**Direct answer:** research published on April 24, 2026 found that including people with disabilities can improve attitudes toward an advertisement, a brand, and a product. The effect appeared across nine pre-registered studies, but disappeared when the representation emphasized vulnerability, impairment, or tokenism. The lesson is clear: visibility without agency can still exclude.

For years, many conversations about diversity in advertising were limited to a single counting question: does a person with a disability appear or not? That is a useful starting point, but an insufficient one. A campaign can add presence while simultaneously reducing someone to inspiration, dependence, or diagnosis.

> Representation does not become inclusive simply by entering the frame. It becomes inclusive when the person retains agency within the story.

## What did Martina Cossu, Zachary Estes, and Joachim Vosgerau investigate?

**Fact:** Martina Cossu, Zachary Estes, and Joachim Vosgerau published the article ["Beyond Visibility: The Disability Inclusion Effect in Advertising"](https://doi.org/10.1177/00222429261447790) in the *Journal of Marketing*. SAGE Publications recorded its online publication on April 24, 2026. The [preprint is available on City Research Online](https://openaccess.city.ac.uk/id/eprint/37335/).

The research program brought together nine pre-registered studies covering both functional and hedonic goods and services. It tested different products, roles within the advertisement—such as customers or models—visible and non-visible disabilities, and responses from consumers with and without disabilities.

The authors report a positive and consistent effect on attitudes toward the advertisement, the brand, and the product. Their explanation is that inclusion can communicate concrete support for the social integration of people with disabilities. The effect appeared even when the presence was driven by an industry regulation, because the audience could perceive a similar social outcome.

## What does the study say, and what does it not allow us to promise?

The research analyzes consumer attitudes. It is not equivalent to demonstrating that any inclusive campaign will increase sales, repurchase, or reputation by a specific amount. Nor does it turn a single advertisement into evidence that an entire company's operations are accessible.

**Our interpretation:** the finding should not be used as a new shortcut to "make inclusion sell." Its value lies in showing that audiences distinguish between a person integrated into the narrative and a person used as an emotional device. That difference has brand consequences, but it first has human consequences.

The article's own abstract marks the most important limit: the effect disappeared when the representation highlighted vulnerability or impairment rather than agency and social inclusion, and when the brand made the model's disability too conspicuous in a tokenizing way.

## What does agency look like inside a campaign?

Agency means the character desires, decides, works, buys, makes mistakes, participates, or leads. Disability is part of their experience, but it does not exhaust their identity or require turning the scene into a moral lesson for the rest of the audience.

### The product has a real function in the story

A person can choose a financial service, recommend a tool, or plan a trip without the camera treating every action as a feat. If the campaign only asks for admiration at the performance of an everyday activity, the brand renders extraordinary a life it claimed to normalize.

### Support does not erase autonomy

Showing assistance, technology, or adjustments is not inherently problematic. The problem arises when support replaces the person's voice and decision-making. A good narrative can acknowledge interdependence without turning someone into an object of care.

### Disability does not appear as a dramatic plot twist

If revealing a non-visible disability serves only to surprise, produce pity, or rehabilitate a brand's reputation, the representation once again revolves around an outside gaze. The right editorial question is what role the person plays, not how much emotional impact their diagnosis produces.

## Four signs of tokenism a team can detect before publishing

**First: the person only represents "overcoming."** They have no motivation, craft, or judgment of their own; they exist to inspire others.

**Second: the advertisement underscores too heavily that it is being inclusive.** The framing, copy, and editing point to the disability as though the brand needs to ensure it receives credit.

**Third: no one with a disability participated in the decisions.** Hiring talent in front of the camera without opening space in the brief, production, creative direction, and review leaves power exactly where it was.

**Fourth: the piece talks about inclusion, but cannot be used.** A video without subtitles, a landing page incompatible with keyboard navigation, or an inaccessible activation contradicts the campaign at the very next click. ## What should change in the marketing process?

The correction does not begin at the end, when only the copy remains to be reviewed. It begins in the brief: who makes decisions, which stereotypes could be reinforced, which formats must be accessible, and how the participation of people with lived experience will be compensated.

During casting and production, it is worth avoiding placing the entire burden of representing all disability on a single person. Adjustments should also be offered for auditions, call times, contracts, spaces, and materials. An inclusive story produced through an excluding process is an inconsistency that cannot be fixed in editing.

Before launching, review narrative and access separately. The first question is whether the representation shows agency and context. The second is whether subtitles, audio description where applicable, contrast, alternative text, keyboard navigation, and the digital destination allow the message to be received. ## Our position: a campaign does not compensate for an inaccessible experience

The study provides a useful signal for brands: representation with agency can generate a broadly favorable response. It would be convenient to stop there. The reality is that a campaign also creates an expectation that the product, the website, customer service, and employment must sustain.

If the advertisement shows full participation, but the purchasing process blocks screen readers or the company does not offer adjustments to candidates, the promise lasts only as long as it takes the audience to reach the next touchpoint. Advertising can open a symbolic door. Accessibility decides whether that door leads anywhere.

**A hypothesis to test:** brands that connect representation, accessible production, and customer experience will achieve a more sustainable response than those that treat disability as an isolated execution. That hypothesis requires their own data: user testing, qualitative listening, journey accessibility, and business results over time.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### What did the study on inclusive advertising find?

Across nine pre-registered studies, including people with disabilities was associated with more favorable attitudes toward the advertisement, the brand, and the product.

### When did the effect disappear?

When the representation emphasized vulnerability or impairment, or highlighted disability in a conspicuous and tokenizing way.

### Does the research guarantee better sales?

No. It analyzed consumer attitudes; it does not allow for a promise of universal sales increases.

### What does showing agency mean?

Giving the character real decisions, motivations, and participation, without reducing their identity to disability, dependence, or inspiration.

### How can tokenism be avoided?

By involving people with disabilities from the brief stage, reviewing stereotypes, showing complete roles, and ensuring that all formats are accessible.
