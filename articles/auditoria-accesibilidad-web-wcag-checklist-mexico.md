# Web Accessibility Audit: WCAG Checklist for Companies in Mexico

**Direct answer:** a web accessibility audit must review at minimum semantic structure, keyboard navigation, visible focus, contrast, alternative text, forms, errors, documents, screen-reader compatibility, and critical user tasks. The most widely used reference is WCAG 2.2, but the business criterion is simpler: can a person complete what they came to do without having to ask for extra help?

Many companies come to this topic when someone asks "are we accessibility-compliant?" in a meeting. The temptation is to open an automated tool, export a nice-looking PDF, and breathe easy. That works as a first step, but not as a complete answer. Accessibility lives in details that a scanner does not fully understand: a button that says "more," a form that does not explain errors, a modal that traps focus, an unreadable PDF, or an image containing important text with no alternative.

> A good audit does not look for culprits. It looks for barriers, impact, and a remediation path that the team can actually execute.

## What a web accessibility audit is

A web accessibility audit is a technical, visual, and functional review of a website to identify obstacles that affect people with visual, auditory, motor, cognitive, or language disabilities, as well as older adults and users in difficult contexts: mobile, low connectivity, time pressure, noise, or low light.

WCAG organizes accessibility around four principles: content must be perceivable, operable, understandable, and robust. Translated into everyday terms: it can be perceived, navigated, understood, and used with different technologies. Inklusion approaches this from a broader vision of 360-degree inclusion: web accessibility, universal design, human resources, and consulting to remove barriers in communication, education, transportation, employment, and services.

## When it makes sense to audit a site

Some moments are obvious: before a redesign, when launching a campaign landing page, after migrating a CMS, when creating a customer portal, or when a legal or compliance team requests evidence. But it also makes sense to audit when metrics show unusual symptoms: high traffic and few form submissions, high bounce rates on key pages, registration abandonment, or user complaints that they "can't find" something that is supposedly visible.

Accessibility should not wait for a crisis. Like technical SEO or security, it becomes more expensive when corrected late.

## WCAG checklist for a serious first review

### 1. Structure and headings

Check that each page has a single clear purpose, one descriptive `h1`, and a logical hierarchy of `h2` and `h3` elements. If headings are used only to "make text bigger," the site loses its mental map for screen readers, search engines, and users who scan.

### 2. Keyboard navigation

Everything important must be usable with a keyboard: menus, buttons, links, forms, carousels, modals, and CTAs. The basic test is simple: mentally disconnect the mouse and use Tab, Shift+Tab, Enter, Esc, and arrow keys. If you get lost, a user will too.

### 3. Visible focus

Focus is the signal that says "you are here." It must be visible and have sufficient contrast. Removing the outline for aesthetic reasons is like erasing the exit sign because it clashes with the wall. It looks clean, but it is a bad idea.

### 4. Contrast and legibility

Validate contrast between text and background, font size, spacing, and readability on mobile. Light grey text may look elegant on a design monitor but fail in real life: low-brightness screens, direct sunlight, low vision, or eye fatigue.

### 5. Alternative text for images

Decorative images can be marked as decorative. Images that convey information need useful alternative text. The goal is not to insert keywords, but to explain what a person needs to know if they cannot see the image.

### 6. Understandable links and buttons

Avoid "click here," "see more," or "read" without context. A link must be understandable on its own: "Request a web accessibility diagnosis" is better than "Submit." This improvement helps accessibility, analytics, internal SEO, and conversions.

### 7. Forms and error messages

Every field needs a clear label, instructions where necessary, specific error messages, and a simple recovery path. "Error" is not helpful. "Enter an email in the format name@domain.com" is. An inaccessible form does not only exclude people: it also loses prospects.

### 8. Multimedia content and documents

Videos, audio, PDFs, presentations, and downloadable files also count. Review captions, transcripts, operable controls, reading order, PDF tags, and alternatives when a document is not accessible.

### 9. Screen readers and assistive technologies

Test with a screen reader on critical flows. It is not enough for things to "look right." They must be announced correctly: button names, menu states, errors, tables, forms, and dynamic changes.

### 10. Critical business tasks

The final question is practical: can a person complete the main actions? Contact, request a quote, purchase, schedule, register, download, log in, or request information. If a barrier appears in these tasks, its priority goes up.

## How to prioritize findings without overwhelming the team

An audit can uncover 20, 80, or 300 findings. The number is impressive, but it does not decide anything. Prioritize using four criteria:

- **Severity:** does it completely block a task or only make it harder?
- **Scope:** does it affect an entire template or a single isolated page?
- **Business impact:** does it occur during contact, sales, registration, support, or transactions?
- **Effort:** can it be fixed through content, design, front-end, CMS, or deep development?

The best report is not the longest one. It is the one that enables decisions: what gets fixed this week, what goes into the next sprint, what requires a redesign, and what must become a standard to prevent recurrence.

## What evidence a company should keep

So that accessibility does not depend on memory or goodwill, document minimum evidence:

- URL reviewed and audit date.
- Related WCAG criterion where applicable.
- Description of the problem in plain language.
- Screenshot, short video, or steps to reproduce.
- Affected users and impacted task.
- Concrete remediation recommendation.
- Owner, priority, target date, and status.
- Follow-up validation: who reviewed it and when.

This documentation helps marketing, technology, legal, customer service, and leadership teams. It also prevents the classic "but we already fixed that," a phrase that tends to appear just before discovering it broke again.

## Why an audit also strengthens SEO and AEO

Accessibility improves the foundation that search engines and answer engines need to understand a site: clear structure, descriptive content, logical navigation, labels, entities, and concrete answers. For SEO, this helps with crawling, indexing, experience, and conversion. For AEO and AI Overviews, it helps content be easy to cite in direct answers.

An accessible article or service page should not hide what matters. It must answer questions such as: what it is, who it applies to, how it is implemented, what errors are common, how long a review takes, what evidence is delivered, and when it makes sense to seek expert support.

## Common errors in accessibility audits

- **Relying only on automated tools:** useful, but incomplete.
- **Auditing only the home page:** serious problems are usually found in forms, landing pages, portals, and documents.
- **Reporting without prioritizing:** a list without impact or owners becomes corporate decoration.
- **Fixing once and forgetting:** accessibility changes every time content or code changes.
- **Separating accessibility from business:** if it is not connected to real tasks, no one defends it when there is time pressure.

## 30-day plan to get started

1. **Days 1–3:** choose 5 to 10 critical pages or flows. Do not try to audit the entire universe in one afternoon.
2. **Days 4–8:** run an automated review and capture errors repeated across templates.
3. **Days 9–14:** test keyboard, focus, contrast, forms, and mobile content.
4. **Days 15–20:** review screen reader behavior and important downloadable documents.
5. **Days 21–25:** prioritize findings by severity, scope, business impact, and effort.
6. **Days 26–30:** fix blockers, re-validate, and turn learnings into an internal checklist.

## Frequently asked questions

### What is a web accessibility audit?

It is a review of pages, components, documents, and flows to find usage barriers. It combines WCAG, automated tools, manual testing, keyboard testing, screen reader evaluation, and assessment of real tasks.

### How often should an audit be conducted?

It depends on the pace of changes. At a minimum, it is advisable to review after redesigns, migrations, new campaigns, CMS changes, or form launches. On active sites, accessibility should be incorporated into the normal QA cycle.

### Is WCAG AA enough?

WCAG AA is generally a reasonable reference for most organizations, but it should not be treated as a ceiling. The goal is for people to be able to use the service. Sometimes that requires content, support, and design decisions that go beyond the checklist.

### Can an automated tool certify my site?

Not on its own. It can detect errors and speed up reviews, but conformance requires manual evaluation and expert judgment about context, tasks, and real-world experience.

## Conclusion

Auditing web accessibility is not about filling in a table to comply. It is about looking at the site honestly: what barriers exist, who they affect, what they cost, and how they are corrected. When done well, an audit improves inclusion, user experience, SEO, conversion, and trust.
