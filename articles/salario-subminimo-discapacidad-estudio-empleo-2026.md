# Eliminating the Subminimum Wage Did Not Cause the Predicted Labor Collapse: What the Transition Actually Demands

**Direct answer:** a study published in June 2026 analyzed 15 U.S. states that eliminated subminimum-wage employment for people with disabilities. It found no significant aggregate decline in employment, hours, or wages. The lesson is not that banning a practice is sufficient: the transition works better when supported employment, coaching, and real opportunities exist.

For decades, the most repeated argument was a practical one: if the below-minimum wage is eliminated, many people will lose their jobs. The new evidence does not confirm that collapse. It also leaves a less comfortable warning: a positive average can conceal those who did not find an alternative.

> Wage dignity does not compete with inclusion. What competes with both is a transition without supports or accountability.

## What Did the Research Published in Labour Economics Study?

**Fact:** Michelle Yin, Regina Seo, and Hoa Vu published ["The labor market effects of subminimum wage elimination: Evidence from a national analysis"](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.labeco.2026.102884) in volume 100 of *Labour Economics*, dated June 2026. The research examined administrative data from the Department of Labor and the Current Population Survey from 2009 to 2024 across 15 states that eliminated subminimum-wage employment.

In the United States, Section 14(c) of the Fair Labor Standards Act allows certain certified employers to pay workers with disabilities less than the federal minimum. The [U.S. Department of Labor](https://www.dol.gov/agencies/whd/special-employment) maintains public information on that regime. It is important to define the scope: the article studies a U.S. policy; it does not interpret Mexican legislation or prove that both labor markets function the same way.

According to the reporting of results published by [Disability Scoop on August 11, 2026](https://www.disabilityscoop.com/2026/08/11/these-places-banned-subminimum-wage-heres-what-happened-to-workers-with-disabilities/32016/), subminimum-wage employment declined by approximately 2,000 workers per state within two years of the change. There was no significant aggregate decline in employment, hours worked, or wages, while income from public programs fell by more than 12%.

## Why Does the Result Matter for Workplace Inclusion?

Because it shifts the burden of proof. It is no longer sufficient to claim that paying less protects jobs. In the states analyzed, the feared outcome did not materialize in general terms. **Our interpretation:** an organization should not confuse an available position with inclusion if the person remains segregated, poorly compensated, or without a path for growth.

This connects to a question Mexico also needs to ask itself, even though the legal framework is different: do we measure inclusion by the number of people who enter, or by the quality of employment they can sustain? Hiring without reviewing salary, duties, supports, training, and internal mobility produces a presentable figure and a fragile experience.

The [Inklusion analysis of the cost of excluding talent with disabilities in Mexico](https://inklusion-blog.vercel.app/blog/costo-exclusion-laboral-discapacidad-mexico-2026/) addresses the local economic dimension. This study adds another piece: raising the compensation standard does not automatically mean withdrawing opportunities.

## What Does the Average Not Allow Us to Conclude?

The lead author herself noted that aggregate results may reflect two movements. People with greater work experience could transition to competitive employment; some people with significant disabilities and less experience outside segregated settings could exit employment. In other words, the average not falling does not mean no one was left behind.

States that accompanied the change with supported employment, job counseling, and vocational rehabilitation showed better transition outcomes, according to Yin's explanation. Here is the part that a rushed policy can omit: closing one pathway without building the next is not inclusion; it is administrative change.

**Hypothesis for Mexican organizations:** teams that combine accommodations, leadership support, accessible training, and career pathways will retain talent better than those that only set a hiring quota. The hypothesis must be verified through retention, progression, and the lived experience of the people involved—it should not be taken as given.

## What Concrete Decisions Does This Leave for Companies?

### Review the Value of the Role, Not the Label on the Person

Define essential functions, outcomes, and salary band before meeting the candidate. If the salary changes when a disability becomes known, the organization must explain what changed about the job—not what it assumes about the person.

### Budget Supports as Talent Infrastructure

Job coaching, assistive technology, workplace adjustments, transportation, or schedule changes can all be part of a transition. Our [guide to reasonable adjustments at work](https://inklusion-blog.vercel.app/blog/ajustes-razonables-trabajo-discapacidad-mexico/) explains how to receive requests and verify whether a solution works without requiring unnecessary disclosure.

### Design Mobility, Not Just Entry

A person hired into a role with no access to training, visible projects, or promotion remains in a narrow lane. Review which positions concentrate employees with disabilities and whether growth opportunities are distributed in a comparable way.

### Listen Without Turning One Voice Into Universal Representation

Combine aggregate data with accessible, confidential interviews. Needs vary by person, task, and context. The goal is not to find "the disability experience," because no single one exists; it is to identify concrete, correctable barriers.

## How to Measure Inclusive Employment Without Hiding Anyone in the Average?

It is worth tracking hiring, comparable compensation, hours, retention, accommodation requests addressed, access to training, promotions, and departures. Segment data when it is ethically and statistically responsible to do so, protect personal data, and avoid publishing groups so small that an individual could be identified.

Include quality measures: autonomy to carry out tasks, relationship with the team, recurring barriers, and capacity to influence decisions. A stable overall rate can coexist with the departure of those who require more support. That is why the dashboard should show transitions and not only a headcount of positions.

## The Position: Dignified Wages and Supports Must Be Discussed at the Same Table

The research by Yin, Seo, and Vu weakens a false choice: pay less or lose jobs. Their national evidence did not find the aggregate collapse that was used for years as a warning. Even so, it would be irresponsible to turn the result into an automatic prescription for another country or into proof that every person made a positive transition.

A mature decision requires two simultaneous commitments: abandoning structures that value work less because of disability, and funding the supports that enable people to participate, remain, and grow. If a company fulfills only the first, it risks leaving someone out. If it only offers supports but normalizes lower pay or a lesser future, it preserves the barrier with better packaging.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### What did the study on subminimum wages find?

It found no significant aggregate decline in employment, hours, or wages after elimination in 15 states; that type of employment did decline.

### Does it prove that no one was left out?

No. Averages can conceal the fact that people with greater support needs exited employment.

### Do the results apply legally to Mexico?

No. They are comparative evidence from the United States, not an interpretation of Mexican law.

### What is integrated competitive employment?

Work alongside people with and without disabilities, with comparable compensation, opportunities, and relevant supports.

### What should a company measure?

Salary, hours, retention, accommodations, training, promotions, autonomy, and corrected barriers.
