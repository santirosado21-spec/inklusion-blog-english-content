# Excluding People with Disabilities from Employment Costs Mexico 251 Billion Pesos a Year: What Companies Must Change

**Direct answer:** an analysis by Éntrale published in July 2026 estimates that Mexico fails to generate 251 billion pesos per year—0.75% of GDP—by excluding people with disabilities from employment. The figure transforms a rights issue into a concrete economic decision: companies must find and remove the barriers that push talent out before, during, and after hiring.

The calculation does not replace the human rights argument. It makes the issue impossible to file away as a peripheral topic of social responsibility. If an organization filters out capability through an inaccessible platform, an inflexible interview, or assumptions about productivity, it loses talent and also contributes to widening a gap that already has macroeconomic dimensions.

## What Did the Éntrale Analysis Find?

[Éntrale, Alianza por la Inclusión Laboral de Personas con Discapacidad](https://entrale.org.mx), is an initiative of the Consejo Mexicano de Negocios. According to coverage published by [La Jornada on July 28, 2026](https://www.jornada.com.mx/noticia/2026/07/28/economia/mexico-pierde-251-mil-mdp-anuales-por-excluir-a-personas-con-discapacidad-del-empleo-estudio) and expanded by [Xataka México on July 31](https://www.xataka.com.mx/empresas-y-economia/mexico-pierde-251-mil-millones-pesos-al-ano-razon-que-va-alla-economia-excluir-a-personas-discapacidad-empleo), the analysis used data from the Instituto Nacional de Estadística y Geografía (INEGI).

**Reported finding:** the labor force participation rate considered was 71.8% among people without disabilities and 56.4% among people with disabilities, a difference of nearly 15 percentage points. The exercise estimates that approximately 730,000 people with disabilities of working age remain outside the labor force due to disability-related barriers rather than age.

The coverage also notes that approximately 90% of the gap is not explained by education, age, sex, or region. The cost of that lost participation was calculated at roughly 251 billion pesos per year, equivalent to 0.75% of GDP.

One important clarification is necessary: the figure is an economic estimate, not money that any single company can "recover" immediately. Nor does it demonstrate that every point of the gap has a single cause. It does provide sufficient evidence to stop treating exclusion as a matter with no productive effect.

## Why Are Losses of Between 3% and 7% of GDP Also Mentioned?

In May 2026, [El Economista interviewed Fernando Estrada Franco, executive director of Éntrale](https://www.eleconomista.com.mx/capital-humano/exclusion-laboral-personas-discapacidad-merma-7-pib-entrale-20260513-813217.html), who referred to broader estimates of between 3% and 7% of GDP for the impact of excluding people with disabilities. That figure and the 0.75% calculation should not be conflated as if they measured exactly the same thing.

The 0.75% published in July corresponds to a specific national exercise on lost labor force participation. The 3%–7% range cited in May is a reference to a broader impact, and its coverage does not present the same methodology. Our position is straightforward: for decision-making purposes, it is better to use the more precisely bounded calculation and retain the methodological note, rather than choosing the larger percentage because it looks better on a slide.

## Our Reading: Hiring Is Not Enough If the System Keeps Pushing People Out

**Editorial interpretation:** the primary value of the study is not in putting a price on dignity. It lies in showing that the barrier has an operational owner. A vacancy has a person responsible for it; an applicant tracking system has a vendor; an interview has a design; an accommodation has a deadline; a promotion has criteria. "Culture" stops being a vague abstraction when each decision is examined.

A company can announce ten hires and still be excluding people if a hundred candidates could not complete the application, if those who were hired did not receive compatible tools, or if none of them reached decision-making positions. Workplace inclusion is measured as a journey, not as a snapshot taken on the day of hiring.

It is also important to separate essential functions from habit. Requiring that a candidate "have excellent verbal communication skills" for a role whose actual task is data analysis may exclude people without improving the selection. Requiring daily in-person attendance when the output is delivered digitally may be another barrier. The standard for the role should be high; what needs to be reduced is the noise that does not actually measure that standard.

## What Should Reach the Leadership Dashboard?

The national figure does not become actionable until it is translated into indicators drawn from the organization's own processes. Leadership and Human Resources should review, with privacy safeguards and voluntary participation:

- **Funnel accessibility:** whether job postings, the careers portal, assessments, and communications work with a keyboard, screen reader, zoom, and alternative formats.
- **Accommodation requests:** response time, implementation, and effectiveness—without treating a low number of requests as an automatic sign of success.
- **Conversion by stage:** application, interview, offer, hiring, and onboarding, to identify where participation drops off.
- **Retention and development:** turnover, training, performance reviews, promotions, and internal mobility—not just new hires.
- **Experience and trust:** sense of belonging, psychological safety to report barriers, and perception of fair treatment.
- **Recurring barriers:** repeated problems that should be resolved for everyone rather than processed as individual exceptions.

Disability data is sensitive. Measuring requires explaining the purpose, limiting access, avoiding samples that could identify individuals, and not making disclosure a condition for receiving dignified treatment. A careless dashboard can create the very barrier it was meant to observe.

## Five Decisions a Company Can Make This Quarter

1. **Test the recruitment process end to end.** Reviewing the careers page is not enough. Someone must complete an application, upload documents, take assessments, and receive communications using different modes of interaction. Our [inclusive recruitment guide](https://inklusion-blog.vercel.app/blog/reclutamiento-incluyente-personas-discapacidad-mexico/) offers a starting point.
2. **Publish an accommodations channel from the first point of contact.** It should appear in the job posting, the interview invitation, onboarding, and throughout employment. [Managing reasonable accommodations](https://inklusion-blog.vercel.app/blog/ajustes-razonables-trabajo-discapacidad-mexico/) requires a designated owner, a timeline, and follow-up.
3. **Rewrite job profiles.** Separate essential outcomes from historical preferences. Remove physical, sensory, or format-based requirements that do not correspond to the actual work.
4. **Review technology and spaces before hiring.** A person should not have to wait weeks for access to the software, the building, or the training they already need in order to do their job.
5. **Measure retention, not campaigns.** A post on the International Day of Persons with Disabilities can accompany the work. It cannot substitute for it. The real indicator of progress is that people join, stay, learn, and advance.

**Operational hypothesis:** companies that measure barriers by stage and correct them as system failures will gain more consistent access to talent than those that rely on isolated campaigns. This must be verified through conversion rates, retention, development, and experience data—not assumed on the basis of good intentions.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### How much does the labor exclusion of people with disabilities cost Mexico?

Éntrale estimates approximately 251 billion pesos per year, equivalent to 0.75% of GDP, in lost labor force participation. It is an economic estimate published in July 2026.

### How many people are estimated to be outside the labor force due to these barriers?

The analysis coverage estimates approximately 730,000 people with disabilities of working age.

### Does the figure prove that the entire gap is caused by discrimination?

No. It reports that approximately 90% of the gap is not explained by variables such as education, age, sex, or region. That points to structural barriers, but does not identify a single cause in every case.

### What should a company review first?

The complete talent journey: job posting, application, interviews, accommodations, hiring, onboarding, tools, training, performance review, promotion, and retention.

### Does inclusion mean lowering standards?

No. It means preserving essential functions and removing barriers that do not measure capability or performance.

## The National Loss Begins with Small Decisions

The 251 billion pesos is a warning signal, not a commercial target. No single company will close the country's gap on its own. Every company can stop producing that gap within its own processes.

The concrete decision is to examine where talent is being lost and assign someone to fix it. When a barrier has evidence, an owner, and a deadline, inclusion stops being a corporate promise and becomes work.
