# Disability Employment Inclusion Needs Public Results, Not Another Corporate Promise

**Direct answer:** in July 2026 the Grattan Institute proposed that large Australian employers publicly report representation, referrals from employment programs, and sub-minimum wages paid to people with disabilities. The proposal does not apply legally to Mexico, but it leaves a useful lesson: workplace inclusion must be measured through hiring, retention, compensation, development, and experience—without forcing the disclosure of personal data.

Many companies publish inclusion commitments. Few explain how many people stay, are promoted, receive reasonable adjustments on time, or leave the organisation because of an avoidable barrier. The problem is not the absence of a more inspiring statement; it is the absence of evidence that makes it possible to distinguish progress from window dressing.

> What is not measured can go unnoticed. What is measured poorly can end up penalising the very people it was meant to include.

## What does the *Opening Doors* report propose?

**Fact:** the [Grattan Institute published *Opening doors: Better jobs and fairer wages for disabled workers* on 26 July 2026](https://grattan.edu.au/report/opening-doors-better-jobs-and-fairer-wages-for-disabled-workers/), an analysis of the Australian employment system for people with disabilities who have high support needs.

The report notes that the employment rate for Australians with profound or severe disability is 34%, compared with 82% for people without disability. It also documents that the Australian Public Service reached 5.9% representation in 2025, below its 7% target. These are Australian figures; they do not describe the Mexican labour market and must not be transposed as though they were equivalent.

Among its recommendations, the institute calls on large employers to publicly report the number of employees with disabilities, disclosure rates, referrals from specialist programs, and the use of sub-minimum wages. It also proposes strengthening open employment, evidence-based supports, fair remuneration, and progression opportunities.

## Why does it matter for an organisation to publish results?

Because a commitment without a baseline makes it impossible to know whether anything changed. A company can increase hiring and, at the same time, lose the majority of those people within the first year. It can offer adjustments but take so long to resolve them that they arrive after the person has already resigned. It can also achieve overall representation while concentrating all talent with disabilities in roles with no mobility.

**Our interpretation:** reporting is not the ultimate goal; it is a tool that allows leadership, HR, and area managers to stop managing inclusion by anecdote. Transparency forces more useful questions: at which stages does the gap appear? Which team corrects barriers? Which outcome improved?

The opposite risk also exists. An isolated figure can become a trophy or a quota. That is why our [analysis of the cost of excluding talent with disabilities](https://inklusion-blog.vercel.app/blog/costo-exclusion-laboral-discapacidad-mexico-2026/) must be complemented by indicators of employment quality, not just a headcount of hires.

## What should a serious workplace inclusion dashboard include?

### Entry: real access to the process

Measure applicants, progression between stages, requests for adjustments, and response times. If the vacancy system is not accessible, or if an assessment eliminates candidacies because of its format, the final representation figure arrives too late to explain where talent was lost. [Inklusion's inclusive recruitment guide](https://inklusion-blog.vercel.app/blog/reclutamiento-incluyente-personas-discapacidad-mexico/) details the barriers worth reviewing before interpreting the numbers.

### Quality: work, salary, and supports

Compare remuneration by level and function, access to tools, adjustments implemented, and time to resolution. Do not publish diagnoses. Publish whether operations are delivering: requests answered, barriers corrected, and pay gaps investigated.

### Trajectory: retention and growth

Track retention at 6, 12, and 24 months, training, promotions, mobility, and departures. A hire that cannot become a career is still a narrow door.

### Experience: safety to speak up

Use accessible surveys and voluntary interviews to find out whether people can request an adjustment, report a barrier, or question a decision without fear of consequences. Segment only when sample size protects individual identity.

## How to measure without turning disability into surveillance?

Disclosure must be voluntary and have a clear purpose. The organisation needs to explain what data it is requesting, how it will be used, who will have access, and how long it will be retained. Information used to manage an adjustment should not circulate as an input for performance evaluation.

Reports must be aggregated. When a group is small, publishing percentages by area, location, and type of disability can identify an individual even if their name does not appear. Define minimum thresholds, restrict access, and allow a "prefer not to answer" option. The non-response rate is also information: it may signal distrust, but it does not by itself prove why that distrust exists.

**Hypothesis:** an organisation that first demonstrates how it protects and uses data will obtain more useful information than one that begins by demanding declarations. This must be verified through voluntary participation, feedback, and a privacy audit.

## What can a Mexican company take from this—and what cannot it?

The report analyses Australian policies, wages, and programmes. It does not establish a legal obligation for Mexico, nor does it substitute for labour or data-protection legal advice. What is transferable is the management discipline: set a baseline, assign responsible parties, measure results, and correct processes.

A Mexican company can begin internally with a quarterly dashboard, process objectives, and executive review. If it decides to publish, it must accompany every figure with scope, period, methodology, and limitations. "We increased by 20%" says little if we do not know from what baseline, over what period, or what experience the people involved actually had.

## Our position: counting people is only the beginning

The Grattan Institute is right about one central point: employment outcomes need visibility. Without data, a policy can consume budget for years without improving access, salary, or progression. But a poorly designed reporting obligation can also reward volume and increase pressure on people to disclose a disability.

The answer is not to choose between transparency and privacy. It is to design both. For a company, that means measuring the full journey, protecting those who share information, and accepting that an uncomfortable figure is more useful than a flawless campaign. Inclusion matures when the team can explain which barrier it eliminated and what changed for the people involved.

## Frequently asked questions

### What should a company measure?
Representation, hiring, retention, promotions, remuneration, adjustments, and experience—using aggregated data.

### Should it require employees to disclose a disability?
No. Participation must be voluntary, informed, and protected.

### Does a quota demonstrate inclusion?
Not on its own. It measures entry, but not quality, trajectory, or barriers corrected.

### Does the report create obligations in Mexico?
No. It is comparative evidence and an Australian proposal, not Mexican legal advice.

### How is privacy protected?
Through explicit purpose, restricted access, aggregation, and thresholds that prevent individuals from being identified.
