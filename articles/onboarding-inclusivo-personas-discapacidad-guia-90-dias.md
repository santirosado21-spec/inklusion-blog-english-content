# Inclusive Onboarding: How to Design the First 90 Days So Talent Can Actually Work

**Direct answer:** inclusive onboarding ensures that every person can access information, tools, spaces, relationships, and expectations from day one. For people with disabilities, this means offering a confidential adjustments channel before the start date, preparing accessible technology and materials, aligning the manager, reviewing barriers throughout 90 days, and measuring autonomy and experience—not just speed.

Hiring does not end when the offer is signed. That is where an operational test begins: whether the organization can turn a talent decision into real conditions for work. When a screen reader does not work with the system, an orientation session has no captions, or a manager is unaware of an already-approved adjustment, the problem is not individual adaptation. It is coordination.

> "We'll sort it out in the first week" can sound considerate. For someone who cannot log in to the system, it means a week without being able to work.

## What makes an onboarding inclusive?

An inclusive onboarding reduces barriers before they become delays, unnecessary exposure, or unfair evaluations. It does not create a separate program for "special cases." It designs an accessible foundation for everyone and adds individual adjustments when the role, the environment, or the tool requires them.

The [Job Accommodation Network's Workplace Accommodation Toolkit](https://askjan.org/toolkit/index.cfm) brings together practices for employers to manage adjustments at different stages of the employment relationship. The guidance originates in the United States and does not substitute for Mexican legal advice; its value here is operational: defining a process, communicating it, and reviewing whether the solution works.

In Mexico, each company must validate its applicable obligations with specialists. This guide focuses on experience, accessibility, and management decisions. It does not request diagnoses, does not determine rights in any specific case, and does not turn the manager into an improvised medical professional.

## What should happen before the first day?

### Offer adjustments without requiring disclosure of clinical information

Include a confidential channel in the offer or welcome communication: "If you need an adjustment to join us or carry out your responsibilities, tell us what barrier you anticipate and what support works for you." The person can share a functional need without narrating their medical history to the entire team.

Define who receives the request, what information is needed, who authorizes resources, and when a response will be given. If the solution requires more time, agree on a temporary alternative that allows the person to begin meaningful tasks. Administrative silence is not neutral: it leaves the person bearing the cost of the delay.

### Test access—don't just request it

Verify the building, parking, route, reception, elevator, restrooms, and workstation. In the digital environment, check the account, authentication, VPN, email, collaboration tools, HR system, and role-specific tools with the relevant assistive technology. "IT confirmed it's active" does not demonstrate that it is usable.

### Provide information that can actually be used

Share the schedule, names, location, hours, dress code if applicable, and what will happen during the first few days. Offer structured documents, captions or transcripts for video, sufficient contrast, and editable formats. Avoid concentrating the entire orientation in a visual presentation with no alternative.

## How should the first day work?

The first conversation should cover the role, priorities, help channels, and agreed adjustments. The manager needs to know the operational change to apply, but not irrelevant clinical data. For example: "meetings must have a prior agenda and follow-up notes" is a work instruction; the diagnosis does not need to circulate.

Introduce the person to the team without turning them into an educational session on disability. If they want to share something, they should decide what, when, and with whom. General inclusion training is the company's responsibility and must not fall on the new hire.

Assign a support contact for cultural and operational questions, but avoid creating dependency. A buddy complements documentation and leadership; they should not be the only way to access information, request an adjustment, or resolve a barrier.

## What should be reviewed during the first 30 days?

The first week confirms access, tools, communication, and understanding of tasks. Ask privately: "What is preventing you from working?", "What agreed solution still isn't working?", and "Is there a format that would make it easier to participate?" Do not wait for the monthly review if a daily blocker exists.

Between weeks two and four, review workload, priorities, and the relationship with the team. Some barriers only appear during execution: a dashboard that cannot be navigated by keyboard, calls without transcription, constant noise, or instructions that depend on visual cues.

Document each barrier with its impact, the action taken, the person responsible, and the date. Our [guide to reasonable adjustments](https://inklusion-blog.vercel.app/blog/ajustes-razonables-trabajo-discapacidad-mexico/) recommends verifying effectiveness after implementation, because delivering a tool does not guarantee it resolves the functional need.

## What changes between days 31 and 60?

The goal shifts from enabling a start to building autonomy. The manager should give feedback on results using defined criteria, not on how "easy" it was to onboard the person. Distinguish performance from friction: if an inaccessible system delays a task, fix the system before concluding that capability is lacking.

Review participation in meetings, training, decisions, and key relationships. A person may be completing tasks while still being excluded from conversations where projects are assigned. Also verify whether adjustments work in remote work, travel, events, and other contexts that did not come up during the first week.

Connect onboarding to development. If mandatory training is not accessible, the barrier has already moved beyond onboarding and begun to limit career progression. ## What should be decided at 90 days?

Conduct a joint review. Confirm what results the person can produce autonomously, which supports should be maintained, which barriers remain open, and what changed in the process for future hires. The adjustment belongs to operations; it should not depend on one person's memory.

If a solution worked, incorporate it into the standard when it benefits more people: accessible documents, prior agendas, captions, clear instructions, or tool testing before the start date. That way the next onboarding begins from a better foundation rather than from scratch.

Do not close the adjustments channel at the 90-day mark. Roles, teams, technologies, and conditions change. A mature process allows for renewed conversation without turning every request into an awkward exception.

## What metrics reveal whether onboarding is working?

Measure the percentage of access ready before the start date, time from a request to a usable solution, open and closed blockers, attendance at activities, understanding of priorities, progress toward autonomy, the person's experience, and retention at 90 and 180 days.

Do not use "time to productivity" as the only measure. It can penalize someone who received their tools late and reward a process that concealed problems. Accompany the figure with actual availability of access and the date on which each critical barrier was resolved.

**Hypothesis to test:** preparing adjustments and technology before the start date will reduce lost time, escalations, and early attrition. To validate it, compare cohorts, document delays attributable to the process, and ask people which obstacle had the greatest impact.

## Five errors worth stopping

**Waiting until the first day to ask.** Some solutions require purchasing, configuration, or testing.

**Sharing the diagnosis with the team.** Share only what is necessary to apply agreed arrangements and protect confidentiality.

**Assigning a support person as a universal solution.** Personal support does not replace technology, documentation, or accessible spaces.

**Confusing speed with performance.** Evaluate results after enabling comparable conditions.

**Celebrating the hire and forgetting retention.** Inclusion is demonstrated when the person can work, learn, participate, and grow.

## Frequently asked questions

### What is inclusive onboarding?
A process that enables access to information, tools, spaces, relationships, and expectations from the outset, with timely accessibility and adjustments.

### When are adjustments discussed?
Before the start date and throughout the employment relationship, through a voluntary and confidential channel.

### Does the manager need to know the diagnosis?
No. They need to know which operational arrangement to apply and how to seek support to resolve barriers.

### What should be ready on the first day?
Physical and digital access, tools, usable materials, a schedule, designated contacts, and agreed adjustments.

### How is it measured?
Through resolution times, access readiness, closed barriers, autonomy, experience, and retention—not speed alone.
