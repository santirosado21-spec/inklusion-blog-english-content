# Customer Service for People with Disabilities: A Protocol for Companies in Mexico

**Direct answer:** a customer-service protocol for people with disabilities must specify how to receive the person, identify the barrier, offer support without imposing it, communicate information in a usable format, resolve or escalate the request, and record the barrier to prevent it from recurring. It must work in-branch, by phone, email, chat, and digital channels; it also requires training, designated owners, and metrics.

A smile helps, but it does not open a heavy door, make a contract legible, or render a form accessible. Good treatment matters when it is backed by processes that allow people to purchase, ask questions, file complaints, and make decisions autonomously.

> The most useful rule is simple: speak to the person, not to a preconceived idea about their disability.

## What Inclusive Service Is and Why It Is a Business Issue

Inclusive service allows a person to use a service under dignified, safe, and as autonomous conditions as possible. It does not seek to treat people in a patronizing way or to grant privileges. It seeks to remove physical, digital, communication, and attitudinal barriers that the service itself may have created.

For a company, this affects acquisition, conversion, retention, and reputation. If someone cannot enter the premises, understand a promotion, complete a payment, or contact support, the organization did not lose a "special visit": it lost a customer.

The [General Law for the Inclusion of Persons with Disabilities](https://www.diputados.gob.mx/LeyesBiblio/pdf/LGIPD.pdf) defines accessibility as the measures to ensure, on equal terms, access to the physical environment, information, communications, technologies, and other services and facilities open to the public or for public use. The [Convention on the Rights of Persons with Disabilities](https://www.ohchr.org/en/instruments-mechanisms/instruments/convention-rights-persons-disabilities), particularly in its Article 9, also addresses access to the environment, information, communications, and services open to the public.

These frameworks define the objective. The protocol translates that objective into daily conduct. It does not replace a legal review applicable to each organization's specific sector.

## Six Principles the Team Can Actually Remember

1. **Address the person directly.** Even when a companion, interpreter, or family member is present, the customer retains their voice and decision-making capacity.
2. **Ask before helping.** "Do you need assistance?" and "How would you prefer I help you?" work better than grabbing a wheelchair, taking someone's arm, or moving an assistive device.
3. **Do not guess at needs.** Two people with a similar disability may choose different forms of support.
4. **Explain before acting.** Describe the next step, the estimated time, and the available alternatives.
5. **Offer equivalent options.** If one channel does not work, enable another without reducing information, safety, or service quality.
6. **Resolve the barrier, not just the moment.** Recording and correcting prevents the next person from having to start from scratch.

## A Seven-Step Service Protocol

### 1. Receive Without Making Assumptions

Greet the person as you would anyone else. Introduce yourself if that adds useful context and ask how you can help. Do not raise your voice by default, do not simplify language to the point of condescension, and do not make the disability the first topic of conversation.

### 2. Identify the Specific Barrier

The question is not "What disability do you have?" but rather "What do you need to complete this process?" or "What format works best for you?" The answer may be a step-free route, more time, written instructions, having information read aloud, subtitles, a quieter space, or assistance completing a process.

### 3. Confirm the Preference

Summarize what you understood and ask for confirmation: "So, I'll send you the terms by email and we'll continue here — is that right?" This pause prevents well-intentioned but unhelpful assistance. It also allows you to explain real limitations and propose an equivalent alternative.

### 4. Deliver Accessible Information

Use plain language, concrete sentences, and a clear sequence. Digital documents must have selectable text, a heading structure, and descriptive links. Relevant images need alternative text. If the process depends on a website or form, the [web accessibility WCAG AA guide](https://inklusion-blog.vercel.app/blog/accesibilidad-web-wcag-aa-mexico/) explains the basic criteria for making it navigable and understandable.

### 5. Maintain Autonomy and Privacy

Request only the information necessary to provide the service. Avoid questions about diagnoses, treatments, or personal life that do not change the solution. If sensitive information must be shared, find a private space or channel and confirm with whom it may be discussed.

### 6. Resolve or Escalate Without Abandoning

Front-line staff must know what they can adjust and to whom they should escalate. If authorization is required, retain ownership of the case: communicate the timeline, share a case number or contact, and follow up. "Another department handles that" is not a protocol; it is a farewell with an org chart.

### 7. Close and Learn

Ask whether the solution allowed the person to complete their objective. Record the barrier, the support requested, the response given, and any pending correction — without storing unnecessary personal data. A repeated incident is no longer a surprise: it becomes an unaddressed operational decision.

## How to Act in Common Situations

### With a Person Who Is Blind or Has Low Vision

- Introduce yourself and let the person know when you step away or when someone else approaches.
- Offer to read information aloud or send it in an accessible digital format.
- Describe location and direction using concrete references; avoid relying on "over there" or "right here."
- Do not touch the cane, or pet, feed, or distract a guide dog.
- If the person accepts human guidance, allow them to take your arm and anticipate steps, doors, or changes in level.

### With a Person Who Is Deaf or Has a Hearing Disability

- Ask which channel they prefer: writing, lip-reading, Mexican Sign Language, subtitles, or another form of support.
- Speak face-to-face, with adequate lighting and a natural pace; do not shout or exaggerate lip movements.
- Reduce background noise when possible.
- Direct the conversation to the person, not to the interpreter.
- Confirm sensitive details such as dates, amounts, addresses, and terms in writing.

### With a Person with Reduced Mobility

- Keep routes, entrances, elevators, and accessible counters clear at all times.
- Do not lean on a wheelchair or move it without permission.
- Bring the service to a usable area if the usual point has a barrier.
- Allow sufficient time without rushing or assuming that accompaniment is needed.
- Review the complete experience using the [universal design checklist for offices and retail spaces](https://inklusion-blog.vercel.app/blog/diseno-universal-oficinas-comercios-checklist-mexico/).

### With a Person with an Intellectual or Psychosocial Disability or Neurodivergence

- Explain one idea at a time and avoid unnecessary technical jargon.
- Offer written instructions or visual supports when they are helpful.
- Allow time to process and respond; do not automatically finish the person's sentences.
- Reduce stimuli or find a quieter space if the person requests it.
- Verify understanding without testing: "Would you like us to go over the next steps together?"

These are guidelines, not rigid labels. The preference expressed by the person takes precedence over any generic list.

## A Protocol for Every Channel, Not Just Reception

**On-site:** review arrival, entry, queuing, wayfinding, furniture, restrooms, payment, and exit. A ramp does not compensate for an inaccessible queue or a payment terminal that is out of reach.

**By phone:** speak clearly, allow pauses, repeat critical information, and offer written follow-up. Do not require voice calls if a safe and available alternative exists.

**By email or chat:** respond with structure, descriptive links, and accessible files. Avoid images containing text as the sole explanation. Maintain a conversation history so the person does not have to repeat everything at each contact.

**On websites and applications:** test keyboard navigation, visible focus, labels, error messages, zoom, contrast, and compatibility with assistive technology. A [web accessibility audit](https://inklusion-blog.vercel.app/blog/auditoria-accesibilidad-web-wcag-checklist-mexico/) combines automated review with manual and functional testing.

## Five Errors a Protocol Must Prevent

- **Imposed assistance:** touching or moving the person or their assistive devices without authorization.
- **Triangulation:** speaking only with the companion even though the person is actively participating.
- **Single channel:** requiring a phone call, a signature, or a form that offers no accessible alternative.
- **Ownerless promise:** logging the complaint without an assigned owner, deadline, or follow-up.
- **Isolated training:** delivering a course while the building, website, and process continue to block access to the service.

## How to Train the Team Without Turning It Into a Lecture

Training must practice real decisions. Use scenarios involving reception, sales, support, payment, and complaints; ask the team to identify the barrier, formulate the right question, offer options, and escalate. Include operational staff, supervisors, security personnel, vendors, and digital owners.

Provide a brief reference card with useful phrases, actions that require permission, responsible parties, and escalation channels. Then observe interactions and correct. A multiple-choice exam can measure recall; it rarely reveals what someone will do when the queue grows and the system fails.

## What to Measure to Know Whether It Works

- barriers reported by channel and service stage;
- time to respond and deliver an adjustment;
- first-contact resolution;
- abandonment in forms, queues, or transfers;
- escalated cases that received follow-up;
- experience reported by customers with disabilities;
- trained staff and observed performance;
- corrections implemented and recurring barriers.

The goal is not to celebrate a low number of requests. It may simply mean that no one found an accessible channel through which to make them. Combine data with journey testing and direct conversation with people with disabilities.

## A 30-Day Implementation Plan

1. **Days 1–5:** map a priority journey, from arrival or login through to resolution.
2. **Days 6–10:** identify physical, digital, informational, and attitudinal barriers; prioritize those that prevent the service from being completed.
3. **Days 11–15:** draft the protocol with actions, limitations, alternatives, responsible parties, and timelines.
4. **Days 16–20:** test scenarios with the team and, where appropriate on a compensated basis, with people with disabilities.
5. **Days 21–25:** correct channels, formats, and escalation points.
6. **Days 26–30:** publish the internal guide, measure the first cases, and schedule a quarterly review.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### What is a customer-service protocol for people with disabilities?

It is an operational guide for receiving, informing, serving, and following up without discrimination or barriers, offering support according to each person's preference.

### How should assistance be offered?

Ask first whether the person needs support and how they prefer to receive it. Listen, and avoid touching their body, wheelchair, cane, guide dog, or other assistive devices without permission.

### Should you speak with the companion?

The conversation must be directed to the customer. A companion or interpreter may provide support when the person chooses, but does not automatically replace their voice.

### What channels should accessible service include?

It is advisable to offer usable alternatives — in person, by phone, email, chat, or accessible form — and to retain context when switching channels.

### Is training staff enough?

No. Accessible facilities, information, systems, and processes are also required, along with designated owners, escalation paths, and follow-up.

### How is inclusive service measured?

Through reported barriers, adjustment times, resolution rates, abandonment by channel, customer experience, and corrections implemented.

## Conclusion

Inclusive service becomes credible when a person can complete their objective without depending on luck, improvisation, or the one employee who "knows how." A clear protocol transforms respect into operations: ask before helping, offer options, protect autonomy, escalate responsibly, and correct the root cause.

Start with a high-impact journey. Observe it from beginning to end and ask: "What happens if this person cannot see the screen, hear the announcement, climb the step, or complete the form?" The answers are the starting point of the protocol.
